Motivation

Separate uncertainty from prediction mismatch, and establish the mathematical bridge from token loss to distillation and KL regularization. Prerequisites: Step 0.2 and weighted sums.

Prerequisites

  • Read the prerequisite chain stated in the original lesson and this review motivation.

Notation and units

  • H(P,Q)=H(P)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)
  • \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j}=q_j-p_j\quad\text{for fixed normalized targets and }T=1

Assumptions

  • Finite discrete normalized distributions, natural logarithms and convention 0·log(0)=0 by a limit. A positive P event absent from Q gives infinite CE and KL. A fixed teacher means fixed with respect to the student parameters and the specified training context.

Step-by-step derivation

  1. Entropy is the P-weighted surprise. Cross-entropy uses the same P weights but evaluates surprise under Q. Expanding log(p_i/q_i)=log p_i−log q_i gives CE=H(P)+KL(P||Q).
  2. For P=[0.7,0.2,0.1], Q=[0.6,0.3,0.1], the independently computed values are H(P)=0.8018185525, CE=0.8286310068 and forward KL=0.0268124543. Reverse KL is 0.0291491245, confirming asymmetry.
  3. For positive P support, Jensen applied to the convex function −ln gives KL≥−ln(sum_{p_i>0}q_i)≥0. Equality requires the distributions to match. Applying this to uniform Q gives H(P)≤ln(V).
  4. A one-hot target keeps only −ln q_y. For q_y=0.6 this is 0.5108256238. For the mastery example P=[0.5,0.5], Q=[0.8,0.2], forward KL is ln(1.25)=0.2231435513.
  5. Differentiating log-softmax gives ∂log q_i/∂z_j=δ_ij−q_j. Therefore normalized fixed targets yield ∂L/∂z_j=q_j−p_j. A temperature-scaled unmultiplied CE contributes the additional factor 1/T.

    Why this matters

    These quantities distinguish uncertainty within a distribution from disagreement between distributions.

    Prerequisites: Stable softmax and log-softmax, Conditional sequence probability and log likelihood

    Symbols, shapes and assumptions

    p and q are normalized distributions over the same support. Natural logs measure nats. Terms with p=0 contribute zero by a limit; p>0 and q=0 require special handling.

    Derivation or mechanism

    1. Define H(p) as the expectation of -log p under p.
    2. Define CE(p,q) with -log q but the same p weights.
    3. Expand log(p/q) to obtain KL(p||q)=CE(p,q)-H(p).
    H(p)=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i
    \mathrm{CE}(p,q)=-\sum_i p_i\log q_i
    D_{KL}(p\Vert q)=\sum_i p_i\log(p_i/q_i)=\mathrm{CE}(p,q)-H(p)

    Worked numerical

    Use p=[0.7,0.25,0.05] and q=[0.4,0.4,0.2]. The exact fixture outputs show H(p), CE(p,q), forward KL≈0.2049154262 and reverse KL≈0.2414140087. Reversing the arguments changes the answer. For p=[1,0], entropy is 0; against q=[0,1], forward KL is infinite.

    Edge cases and limits

    • KL is not a symmetric distance metric.
    • A zero in q on positive p support cannot be replaced by an arbitrary epsilon without changing the problem.
    • Mode-covering and mode-seeking interpretations require a specified student family and optimization setting; those later claims remain separate audit targets.

    STEP 0.3 — ENTROPY → CROSS-ENTROPY → KL DIVERGENCE

    Plan node: I0 · chat order #4

    This is one of the most important mathematical steps in the entire course.

    You already understand:

    \text{logits}\rightarrow\text{softmax}\rightarrow\text{probabilities}

    Now we need to answer:

    How do we measure whether one probability distribution is good, uncertain, or different from another?

    That gives us:

    \boxed{\text{Entropy}}
    \boxed{\text{Cross-Entropy}}
    \boxed{\text{KL Divergence}}

    And these are not isolated formulas. They form a chain that later becomes:

    \text{LM Training} \rightarrow \text{Knowledge Distillation} \rightarrow \text{RLHF/RLVR} \rightarrow \text{PPO/GRPO}

    Your master cheatsheet explicitly places entropy, cross-entropy, and KL in the probability/information-theory foundation, and later uses KL directly in distillation and RL. (Source: MASTER_Mathematical_Cheatsheet_All_Lecture_PDFs)

    0.3.1 First question: what is entropy trying to measure?

    Suppose a model has two possible outcomes.

    Distribution A
    P=[1,0]

    The model is completely certain.

    Distribution B
    P=[0.5,0.5]

    The model has no preference.

    Intuitively:

    \boxed{\text{Distribution B is more uncertain than Distribution A}}

    Entropy gives us a mathematical way to quantify that uncertainty.

    0.3.2 Entropy

    For a discrete distribution:

    \boxed{ H(P)=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i }

    where:

    • P = probability distribution
    • p_i = probability of outcome i
    • H(P) = entropy

    This formula is part of the mathematical foundation in your master cheatsheet. (Source: MASTER_Mathematical_Cheatsheet_All_Lecture_PDFs)

    0.3.3 Why the minus sign?

    We already learned:

    0<p_i\le1

    therefore:

    \log p_i\le0

    So:

    p_i\log p_i\le0

    Without the minus sign, entropy would generally be negative.

    We want uncertainty to be represented as a nonnegative quantity, so:

    -\sum p_i\log p_i

    is used.

    0.3.4 Perfect certainty

    Take:

    P=[1,0]

    Then:

    H(P) = -[1\log1+0\log0]

    Since:

    \log1=0

    the first term is zero.

    The 0\log0 term is interpreted through its limiting value as zero.

    Therefore:

    \boxed{H(P)=0}

    So:

    A completely certain distribution has zero entropy.

    0.3.5 Maximum uncertainty for two outcomes

    Now:

    P=[0.5,0.5]

    Then:

    H(P) = -[0.5\log0.5+0.5\log0.5]

    Since:

    \log(0.5)\approx-0.6931

    we get:

    H(P)\approx0.6931

    Thus:

    \boxed{ H([0.5,0.5])\approx0.693 }

    which is larger than zero.

    Therefore:

    \boxed{ \text{more spread-out distribution} \Rightarrow \text{higher entropy} }

    0.3.6 Three outcomes

    Consider:

    P=[1/3,1/3,1/3]

    Entropy:

    H(P) = -3\left(\frac13\log\frac13\right)
    = -\log(1/3)
    =\log3

    Using natural logarithms:

    \boxed{H(P)\approx1.099}

    So the maximum entropy increases as more possible outcomes become equally likely.

    For V equally likely outcomes:

    \boxed{H_{\max}=\log V}

    when using natural logarithms.

    0.3.7 The intuition behind entropy

    Think of entropy as:

    How spread out is the probability mass?

    Very concentrated:

    [0.99,0.01]

    → low entropy.

    Very spread out:

    [0.5,0.5]

    → high entropy.

    For a vocabulary of 50,000 tokens, a model assigning almost all probability to one token has relatively low entropy, while a model spreading probability broadly across thousands of alternatives has higher entropy.

    0.3.8 Another interpretation: surprise

    There is another extremely useful interpretation.

    The surprise of observing event i is:

    \boxed{ I(i)=-\log p_i }

    Why?

    Because:

    • highly likely event → small surprise
    • unlikely event → large surprise

    For example:

    p=0.9

    gives:

    -\log(0.9)\approx0.105

    while:

    p=0.01

    gives:

    -\log(0.01)\approx4.605

    Entropy is the expected surprise:

    \boxed{ H(P)=E_{x\sim P}[-\log P(x)] }

    Expanding the expectation gives:

    H(P) = -\sum_x P(x)\log P(x)

    So entropy means:

    If outcomes are actually generated according to P, how much surprise do we expect on average?

    0.3.9 Now the crucial question: what is cross-entropy?

    Entropy talks about:

    the distribution itself.

    Cross-entropy asks:

    What happens when the true distribution is P, but I use another distribution Q to assign probabilities?

    The formula is:

    \boxed{ H(P,Q) = -\sum_i p_i\log q_i }

    Your supplied Hands-On Machine Learning text gives this exact definition and explains that cross-entropy is larger than the entropy of the true distribution when the assumed distribution is imperfect. (Source: Hands-On Machine Learning with Scikit-Lear - Aurelien Geron)

    0.3.10 Why are there TWO distributions?

    This is the key conceptual difference.

    Entropy:

    H(P) = -\sum_i p_i\log p_i

    uses:

    P\rightarrow P

    Cross-entropy:

    H(P,Q) = -\sum_i p_i\log q_i

    uses:

    P\rightarrow Q

    So:

    Quantity Meaning
    H(P) uncertainty inherent in P
    H(P,Q) how costly it is to encode outcomes from P using Q

    0.3.11 Numerical example

    Let's use:

    P=[0.7,0.2,0.1]

    and suppose our model uses:

    Q=[0.6,0.3,0.1]

    First calculate entropy of P:

    H(P) = -[0.7\log0.7+0.2\log0.2+0.1\log0.1]

    Numerically:

    \boxed{H(P)\approx0.802}

    Now cross-entropy:

    H(P,Q) = -[0.7\log0.6+0.2\log0.3+0.1\log0.1]

    giving:

    \boxed{H(P,Q)\approx0.829}

    Notice:

    0.829>0.802

    Why?

    Because Q isn't exactly P.

    0.3.12 The beautiful relationship

    Cross-entropy can be decomposed into:

    \boxed{ H(P,Q)=H(P)+D_{KL}(P\|Q) }

    This identity is explicitly used in the supplied MiniLLM material to explain knowledge distillation. (Source: minillm-slides)

    This equation is tremendously important.

    Let's understand it rather than memorize it.

    0.3.13 What is KL divergence?

    KL divergence measures how different Q is from P, in an information-theoretic sense:

    \boxed{ D_{KL}(P\|Q) = \sum_i p_i \log \frac{p_i}{q_i} }

    Your supplied active-learning material describes KL divergence as measuring the difference between a true distribution P and an approximating distribution Q, and notes that it is zero when the two distributions match.

    0.3.14 Derive the cross-entropy identity

    Start with KL:

    D_{KL}(P\|Q) = \sum_i p_i \log\frac{p_i}{q_i}

    Use:

    \log\frac{a}{b} = \log a-\log b

    Therefore:

    D_{KL}(P\|Q) = \sum_i p_i\log p_i - \sum_i p_i\log q_i

    Now recall:

    H(P)=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i

    and:

    H(P,Q)=-\sum_i p_i\log q_i

    So:

    D_{KL}(P\|Q) = -H(P)+H(P,Q)

    Rearrange:

    \boxed{ H(P,Q)=H(P)+D_{KL}(P\|Q) }

    There is the identity.

    0.3.15 What does this identity actually mean?

    It says:

    \boxed{ \text{cross-entropy} = \text{intrinsic uncertainty} + \text{mismatch penalty} }

    Specifically:

    H(P)

    is unavoidable uncertainty in the original distribution.

    And:

    D_{KL}(P\|Q)

    is the additional cost caused by using the wrong distribution Q.

    This is a fantastic mental model.

    0.3.16 What happens when P=Q?

    Suppose:

    Q=P

    Then:

    D_{KL}(P\|P) = \sum_i p_i\log\frac{p_i}{p_i}
    = \sum_i p_i\log1
    =0

    Therefore:

    H(P,P) = H(P)

    So:

    \boxed{ \text{perfect match} \Rightarrow KL=0 \Rightarrow CE=entropy }

    This exact idea is demonstrated in the supplied MiniLLM slides: when the student exactly matches the teacher, KL is zero but the distillation cross-entropy remains equal to the teacher's entropy. (Source: minillm-slides)

    0.3.17 Let's verify our numerical example

    We had:

    H(P)\approx0.8018

    and:

    H(P,Q)\approx0.8286

    Therefore:

    D_{KL}(P\|Q) = H(P,Q)-H(P)
    \approx0.8286-0.8018
    \boxed{D_{KL}(P\|Q)\approx0.0268}

    So:

    0.8286 = 0.8018+0.0268

    Exactly.

    0.3.18 Important property: KL is nonnegative

    For valid distributions:

    \boxed{ D_{KL}(P\|Q)\ge0 }

    and:

    D_{KL}(P\|Q)=0

    only when:

    P=Q

    under the usual conditions.

    Therefore:

    H(P,Q)\ge H(P)

    This means:

    Using the wrong distribution cannot give you lower cross-entropy than the true distribution's own entropy.

    0.3.19 But KL is NOT a distance

    This is a common interview/exam trap.

    People often casually call KL a "distance," but technically it is a divergence, not a metric.

    Why?

    A metric generally requires symmetry:

    d(P,Q)=d(Q,P)

    But KL generally does not satisfy this.

    Usually:

    \boxed{ D_{KL}(P\|Q)\neq D_{KL}(Q\|P) }

    This asymmetry becomes extremely important later in knowledge distillation.

    0.3.20 Forward KL vs Reverse KL

    There are two directions.

    Forward KL
    \boxed{ D_{KL}(P\|Q) }
    Reverse KL
    \boxed{ D_{KL}(Q\|P) }

    They are not interchangeable.

    Your MiniLLM materials explicitly distinguish these two and discuss their different behavior. (Source: MASTER_Mathematical_Cheatsheet_All_Lecture_PDFs_v2)

    0.3.21 Why direction matters

    Consider P as the teacher and Q as the student.

    Forward KL:

    D_{KL}(P\|Q) = \sum_xP(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}

    The teacher's probabilities P(x) are used as weights.

    So if the teacher assigns meaningful probability to something and the student gives it almost zero, the student gets heavily penalized.

    This tends toward covering teacher-supported modes.

    Your supplied MiniLLM material describes forward KL as strongly penalizing missing teacher-supported modes. (Source: MASTER_Mathematical_Cheatsheet_All_Lecture_PDFs_v2)

    Reverse KL:

    D_{KL}(Q\|P) = \sum_xQ(x)\log\frac{Q(x)}{P(x)}

    Now the student's distribution determines which regions are emphasized.

    It strongly penalizes putting probability mass where the teacher says probability is very low.

    This is associated with mode-seeking behavior in the supplied MiniLLM explanation. (Source: MASTER_Mathematical_Cheatsheet_All_Lecture_PDFs_v2)

    We will return to this in depth when we reach MiniLLM.

    0.3.22 Cross-entropy in language-model training

    Now connect today's mathematics directly to Transformers.

    Suppose vocabulary:

    V=\{\text{the, cat, sat, dog, ran}\}

    Correct token:

    y=\text{sat}

    One-hot target:

    P=[0,0,1,0,0]

    Model distribution:

    Q=[0.05,0.10,0.60,0.05,0.20]

    Cross-entropy:

    H(P,Q) = -\sum_iP_i\log Q_i

    Because all P_i are zero except the correct token:

    = -\log0.60
    \boxed{ H(P,Q)=0.5108 }

    This exact example is in the supplied MiniLLM slides. (Source: minillm-slides)

    So ordinary next-token training is just cross-entropy with a one-hot target.

    0.3.23 Why does one-hot cross-entropy collapse to -\log q_y?

    Suppose:

    y=[0,0,1,0]

    and:

    q=[0.1,0.2,0.6,0.1]

    Then:

    -\sum_i y_i\log q_i

    becomes:

    - [ 0\log0.1+ 0\log0.2+ 1\log0.6+ 0\log0.1 ]

    Everything disappears except:

    \boxed{-\log0.6}

    This is why the ordinary LM loss focuses on the probability assigned to the observed token.

    0.3.24 Now the BIG connection: distillation

    Imagine we replace the hard one-hot target:

    [0,0,1,0,0]

    with the teacher's output:

    P_{\text{teacher}} = [0.02,0.05,0.70,0.03,0.20]

    Now the student might predict:

    Q_{\text{student}} = [0.05,0.10,0.60,0.05,0.20]

    The loss becomes:

    \boxed{ H(P_{\text{teacher}},Q_{\text{student}}) = -\sum_v P_{\text{teacher}}(v) \log Q_{\text{student}}(v) }

    Now every vocabulary item contributes.

    This is the mathematical reason knowledge distillation carries more information than one-hot supervision.

    The supplied MiniLLM example explicitly shows that the teacher's entire probability shape—not just the true token—is used. (Source: minillm-slides)

    0.3.25 Why call this "dark knowledge"?

    Suppose the teacher says:

    P= [0.01,0.02,0.70,0.05,0.22]

    A one-hot label only says:

    sat is correct.

    It loses the teacher's relative judgments among wrong answers.

    But the teacher distribution tells us:

    P(\text{sat})=0.70
    P(\text{ran})=0.22
    P(\text{dog})=0.05

    etc.

    So the student learns:

    sat is preferred, but ran is also somewhat plausible.

    That extra structure is what the distillation material refers to as dark knowledge. (Source: minillm-slides)

    0.3.26 Why temperature enters distillation

    A teacher may produce:

    [0.999,0.0005,0.0003,\ldots]

    That distribution is too sharp to reveal much information about secondary choices.

    Temperature modifies logits before softmax:

    \boxed{ p_i^{(T)} = \frac{e^{z_i/T}} {\sum_j e^{z_j/T}} }

    With:

    T>1

    the distribution becomes softer.

    For example:

    [0.999,0.0005,0.0003]

    might become something more informative such as:

    [0.70,0.20,0.10]

    The exact numbers depend on the logits, but the conceptual point is:

    \boxed{ T>1 \Rightarrow \text{more information in the relative alternatives} }

    The supplied MiniLLM slides explicitly use temperature to soften teacher outputs and expose relative confidence among alternatives. (Source: minillm-slides)

    This will become a whole topic later.

    0.3.27 The same KL appears later in RL

    This is where today's lesson becomes much more powerful.

    In reinforcement learning for language models, we often don't want the updated policy to move arbitrarily far from a reference policy.

    So KL divergence appears as a constraint or penalty.

    Conceptually:

    \boxed{ \text{reward improvement} - \text{penalty for moving too far} }

    One common structure is:

    J(\theta) = E[R] - \beta D_{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})

    The exact objective varies by method, but the mathematical idea is the same:

    \boxed{ KL=\text{distribution mismatch} }

    Your master cheatsheet explicitly places KL in both knowledge distillation and RL sections. (Source: MASTER_Mathematical_Cheatsheet_All_Lecture_PDFs_v2)

    So understanding KL now will make later RL mathematics much easier.

    0.3.28 A unifying view

    We now have:

    Entropy
    \boxed{ H(P)=E_{x\sim P}[-\log P(x)] }

    Interpretation:

    How uncertain/spread out is P?

    Cross-entropy
    \boxed{ H(P,Q)=E_{x\sim P}[-\log Q(x)] }

    Interpretation:

    How much surprise do I incur when data comes from P, but I use Q to predict it?

    KL divergence
    \boxed{ D_{KL}(P\|Q) = E_{x\sim P} \left[ \log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \right] }

    Interpretation:

    How much extra information cost/mismatch arises from using Q instead of P?

    And:

    \boxed{ H(P,Q)=H(P)+D_{KL}(P\|Q) }

    0.3.30 One subtle but VERY important distinction

    Do not confuse these:

    H(P)

    with:

    H(P,Q)

    with:

    D_{KL}(P\|Q)

    Their roles are different.

    Formula What it measures
    H(P) uncertainty of P itself
    H(P,Q) prediction/encoding cost when true distribution is P, model uses Q
    D_{KL}(P\Vert Q) mismatch between P and Q

    And:

    \boxed{ CE = Entropy + KL }

    not:

    CE=KL

    However, when optimizing Q_\theta with P fixed:

    H(P)

    doesn't depend on \theta.

    Therefore:

    \boxed{ \arg\min_\theta H(P,Q_\theta) = \arg\min_\theta D_{KL}(P\|Q_\theta) }

    This is why a distillation cross-entropy objective can be described as equivalent to minimizing forward KL with respect to the student's parameters. The supplied MiniLLM material makes exactly this point. (Source: minillm-slides)

    0.3.32 MASTER NUMERICAL EXAMPLE

    Given:

    P=[0.7,0.2,0.1]
    Q=[0.6,0.3,0.1]

    We found:

    \boxed{H(P)\approx0.8018}
    \boxed{H(P,Q)\approx0.8286}
    \boxed{D_{KL}(P\|Q)\approx0.0268}

    and verify:

    \boxed{ 0.8286\approx0.8018+0.0268 }

    This one example contains the entire relationship.

    0.3.33 Code it

    import torch

P = torch.tensor([0.7, 0.2, 0.1])
Q = torch.tensor([0.6, 0.3, 0.1])

# Entropy H(P)
entropy = -(P * torch.log(P)).sum()

# Cross-entropy H(P, Q)
cross_entropy = -(P * torch.log(Q)).sum()

# KL divergence D_KL(P || Q)
kl_divergence = (P * torch.log(P / Q)).sum()

print("Entropy:", entropy.item())
print("Cross-entropy:", cross_entropy.item())
print("KL divergence:", kl_divergence.item())
print("Entropy + KL:", (entropy + kl_divergence).item())

    You should observe approximately:

    Entropy:          0.8018
Cross-entropy:    0.8286
KL divergence:    0.0268
Entropy + KL:     0.8286

    0.3.34 One more connection to gradients

    You already learned in the previous lesson that we minimize negative log probability.

    Cross-entropy gives us the same training objective:

    L = -\sum_i y_i\log q_i

    where y is the target distribution.

    For a one-hot target:

    L=-\log q_y

    And with softmax outputs, the gradient with respect to a class logit has the particularly elegant form:

    \boxed{ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}=q_i-y_i }

    This is one of the reasons softmax + cross-entropy is so convenient for training neural networks. The supplied Hands-On Machine Learning text derives the corresponding softmax-regression gradient in terms of predicted probabilities minus target indicators. (Source: Hands-On Machine Learning with Scikit-Lear - Aurelien Geron)

    We will derive this carefully in the calculus/optimization foundation step, rather than skipping the derivation.
