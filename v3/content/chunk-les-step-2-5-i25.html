Motivation

Make the full decoding order and sequence-scoring conventions explicit, including history-dependent changes.

Prerequisites

  • Greedy/beam distinction
  • Temperature
  • Top-k and nucleus

Notation and units

  • lp(Y)=((5+length)/6)^alpha
  • s(Y)=log P(Y)/lp(Y)
  • theta>=1 repetition factor

Assumptions

  • EOS/length conventions specified
  • Toy positive-logit penalty is z/theta
  • Sign-aware practical variant divides positive scores, multiplies negative scores

Step-by-step derivation

  1. Keep sequence search (beam) separate from next-token distribution transforms (temperature/filtering/sampling). Beam width counts retained sequences; k counts eligible next tokens.
  2. History transform precedes ranking if its purpose is to change candidate eligibility. For [5,4,2,0],repeat index0,theta2 gives [2.5,4,2,0].
  3. Exact top3 gives [2.5,4,2,-inf], T2 gives [1.25,2,1,-inf], softmax gives [.2566866356,.5434056117,.1999077528,0].
  4. Plain division is not a universally decreasing repetition penalty: -2/2=-1 raises a negative logit. The sign-aware counterpart -2*2=-4 suppresses it; retain source-specific numerical rule but state its scope.
  5. For [.8,.5,.9], P=.36 and lnP=-1.0216512475. For [.7,.95], P=.665 and lnP=-.4079682383, correcting inherited -.4072.

  7. Positive T commutes with exact top-k membership, but top-p depends on the chosen probability distribution. Repetition scaling is not invariant to a common logit shift, so raw-logit convention matters.

Corrections and source qualifications

  • heading: 2.5.37; text: ln(.665)=-.4079682383; four-decimal rounding is -.4080, rather than -.4072.

Edge cases and remaining gaps

  • Length-normalized score is not the raw sequence probability or a calibrated distribution over sequences.
  • Beam search cannot guarantee globally best or highest-quality completion after pruning.
  • Pure greedy reproducibility also needs deterministic model/kernel/constraint/tie behavior.
  • Decoding heuristics must be evaluated on target tasks; source task associations are guidance, not optimality proofs.

Step 2.5 — Putting the Decoding Strategies Together

Plan node: I2 · chat order #25

We now have enough pieces to understand the entire decoding decision process.

So far:

\boxed{\text{Greedy}}
\boxed{\text{Beam Search}}
\boxed{\text{Sampling}}
\boxed{\text{Temperature}}
\boxed{\text{Top-k}}
\boxed{\text{Top-p}}

The important thing now is to understand what problem each solves, where it acts, and how the mathematics fits together.

Your master cheatsheet summarizes the same progression: a language model produces a full vocabulary distribution, and decoding is the process that turns that distribution into the next token; beam search operates on candidate sequences, while temperature/top-k/top-p operate on the next-token distribution. (Source: MASTER_Mathematical_Cheatsheet_All_Lecture_PDFs_v2)

2.5.1 First: What Does the LLM Actually Produce?

Suppose the current context is:

The weather today is

The Transformer does not directly output:

sunny

Instead, the final layer produces logits:

\boxed{ z\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|} }

where every vocabulary token has one logit.

For example:

Token Logit
bright 3.0
sunny 2.5
cold 1.5
rainy 0.2
pizza -2.0

Then:

P=\operatorname{softmax}(z).

The resulting distribution might be:

P=[0.50,0.35,0.10,0.04,0.01].

This distribution is the raw decision space.

Everything we have studied is a different way of deciding what happens next.

2.5.2 The Critical Question

At this point ask:

Do I want to choose one token immediately, or do I want to search through several possible future sequences?

That separates the decoding world into two major families.

Family A — Immediate next-token selection

\boxed{ \text{Greedy / Sampling / Temperature / Top-k / Top-p} }

These operate primarily on the current next-token distribution.

Family B — Sequence-level search

\boxed{ \text{Beam Search} }

Beam search keeps multiple partial sequences alive and explores future continuations.

This distinction is extremely important.

2.5.3 Greedy Decoding

Greedy decoding chooses:

\boxed{ y_t=\arg\max_v P(y_t=v\mid y_{<t},x) }

Suppose:

P= [0.50,0.35,0.15].

Greedy chooses the:

\boxed{0.50}

token.

Then the token is appended.

Then the model runs again.

So:

context
   ↓
model
   ↓
probabilities
   ↓
argmax
   ↓
next token
   ↓
append
   ↓
repeat

The source emphasizes that this is efficient and deterministic, but myopic: it optimizes the immediate decision rather than the overall sequence. (Source: decoding-sampling-slides)

2.5.4 Why Greedy Can Fail

Your slides give this exact idea with:

P(\text{bright})=0.50

and:

P(\text{sunny})=0.35.

Greedy says:

\text{bright}.

But suppose:

P(\text{outside}\mid\text{bright})=0.50

while:

P(\text{outside}\mid\text{sunny})=0.95.

Then:

Bright sequence
P(\text{bright outside}) = 0.50\times0.50 = 0.25.
Sunny sequence
P(\text{sunny outside}) = 0.35\times0.95 = 0.3325.

Therefore:

\boxed{ 0.3325>0.25. }

So the globally better sequence starts with the token that greedy rejected.

The source explicitly uses this example to demonstrate greedy's myopia. (Source: decoding-sampling-slides)

2.5.5 Sequence Probability

For a generated sequence:

Y=(y_1,y_2,\ldots,y_T),

the probability is:

\boxed{ P(Y|X) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(y_t|y_{<t},X) }

This comes from the chain rule.

Because multiplying many probabilities quickly creates very tiny values, we usually take logarithms:

\boxed{ \log P(Y|X) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(y_t|y_{<t},X) }

The master cheatsheet uses exactly this sequence scoring formulation. (Source: MASTER_Mathematical_Cheatsheet_All_Lecture_PDFs_v2)

2.5.6 Why Log Probability Is Better Computationally

Suppose:

P_1=0.8,\quad P_2=0.7,\quad P_3=0.9.

Sequence probability:

0.8\times0.7\times0.9 = 0.504.

Log probability:

\log(0.8)+\log(0.7)+\log(0.9).

Approximately:

-0.2231-0.3567-0.1054 = -0.6852.

The two rankings are equivalent because log is monotonic:

P_A>P_B \iff \log P_A>\log P_B.

So beam search can safely compare:

\boxed{\log P}

instead of tiny probability products.

The decoding slides explicitly mention log-probabilities for avoiding numerical underflow. (Source: decoding-sampling-slides)

2.5.7 Beam Search

Greedy keeps:

\boxed{1}

partial sequence.

Beam search keeps:

\boxed{B}

partial sequences.

The integer:

\boxed{B=\text{beam width}}

controls how many hypotheses survive.

Your source describes the beam process as:

  1. Start with an empty sequence.
  2. Expand every retained sequence.
  3. Score resulting candidates using sequence probability or log-probability.
  4. Keep only the best B.
  5. Repeat until candidates finish. (Source: decoding-sampling-slides)

2.5.8 Why Beam Search Helps

Suppose at step 1:

P(\text{bright})=0.50
P(\text{sunny})=0.35
P(\text{cold})=0.15.

Greedy keeps only:

\boxed{\text{bright}}.

Beam search with:

B=2

keeps:

\boxed{ \text{bright},\text{sunny} }

and removes:

\text{cold}.

Now at the next step, both are expanded.

This gives the second hypothesis a chance to prove itself.

2.5.9 Beam Search Worked Example

Using the same distributions from your slide:

Step 1
Token Probability Status
bright 0.50 keep
sunny 0.35 keep
cold 0.15 prune

because:

B=2.
Step 2

Suppose:

P(\text{outside}\mid\text{bright})=0.50
P(\text{today}\mid\text{bright})=0.30
P(\text{again}\mid\text{bright})=0.20.

For sunny:

P(\text{outside}\mid\text{sunny})=0.95
P(\text{today}\mid\text{sunny})=0.03
P(\text{again}\mid\text{sunny})=0.02.

Now calculate every candidate.

Bright branch
P(\text{bright outside}) = 0.50\times0.50 = 0.25
P(\text{bright today}) = 0.50\times0.30 = 0.15
P(\text{bright again}) = 0.50\times0.20 = 0.10
Sunny branch
P(\text{sunny outside}) = 0.35\times0.95 = 0.3325
P(\text{sunny today}) = 0.35\times0.03 = 0.0105
P(\text{sunny again}) = 0.35\times0.02 = 0.007.

Top two:

\boxed{ 0.3325,\;0.25 }

Therefore final beams:

\boxed{ \text{sunny outside} }

and:

\boxed{ \text{bright outside}. }

The source gives this exact worked example. (Source: decoding-sampling-slides)

2.5.10 The Special Case B=1

If:

B=1,

only one sequence survives after every step.

So:

\boxed{ B=1\Rightarrow\text{greedy decoding} }

This is one of the most useful exam facts.

2.5.11 Why Raw Sequence Probability Has a Problem

Suppose candidate A has:

2

tokens, each with probability:

0.60.

Then:

P(A)=0.60^2=0.36.

Candidate B has:

4

tokens, each with probability:

0.75.

Then:

P(B)=0.75^4 \approx0.3164.

Raw probability says:

\boxed{A>B}

because:

0.36>0.3164.

But B was more confident at every individual token.

This exposes a length bias.

The longer sequence is forced to multiply more numbers below 1.

2.5.12 Length Normalization

Your decoding slide introduces the Google NMT length-normalization formulation from Wu et al.

Define:

\boxed{ lp(Y)= \frac{(5+|Y|)^\alpha} {(5+1)^\alpha} }

and:

\boxed{ s(Y)= \frac{\log P(Y)} {lp(Y)} }

where:

  • |Y| = sequence length,
  • \alpha = strength of the length penalty.

The slide notes that the reported useful range was around:

\alpha\in[0.6,0.7].

(Source: decoding-sampling-slides)

2.5.13 Why Divide by Length Penalty?

Raw log probability becomes more negative as more tokens are added.

For example:

\log0.6\approx-0.5108.

Two such tokens:

2(-0.5108)=-1.0217.

Four 0.75-probability tokens:

4\log0.75 = -1.1507.

Raw scoring says:

-1.0217>-1.1507.

So A wins.

But length normalization changes the score.

For:

\alpha=0.6,

the slide gives approximately:

A
lp(A)\approx1.0969
s(A)=\frac{-1.0217}{1.0969} \approx-0.9314.
B
lp(B)\approx1.2754
s(B)=\frac{-1.1507}{1.2754} \approx-0.9022.

Now:

\boxed{ -0.9022>-0.9314 }

so B wins.

That exact inversion is shown in the slide. (Source: decoding-sampling-slides)

2.5.14 What Is Length Normalization Really Doing?

Think of it as saying:

"Don't punish a sequence solely because it contains more tokens."

It does not simply divide by:

|Y|.

Instead, the slide uses a smoother length penalty:

\frac{(5+|Y|)^\alpha}{6^\alpha}.

So it partially compensates for the systematic decrease in raw sequence probability.

2.5.15 Beam Search + Length Normalization

Therefore, a better beam-search scoring pipeline is:

\boxed{ \text{candidate sequence} \rightarrow \log P(Y) \rightarrow lp(Y) \rightarrow s(Y)=\frac{\log P(Y)}{lp(Y)} }

Then rank beams using:

\boxed{s(Y)}.

2.5.16 The Big Difference Between Beam Search and Top-k

This distinction is essential.

Top-k

Looks at the current vocabulary distribution:

P(y_t|\text{context})

and says:

Keep the top k next-token candidates.

Beam search

Looks at multiple partial sequences:

(y_1,y_2,\ldots,y_t)

and says:

Keep the best B sequence hypotheses.

So:

\boxed{ k=\text{next-token candidate count} }

while:

\boxed{ B=\text{sequence hypothesis count} }

Do not confuse k and B.

2.5.17 Sampling

Now return to stochastic generation.

Sampling means:

\boxed{ y_t\sim P(\cdot|y_{<t},X) }

instead of:

\arg\max.

Suppose:

P=[0.6,0.3,0.1].

Over many independent draws:

  • first token appears roughly 60% of the time,
  • second roughly 30%,
  • third roughly 10%.

This introduces diversity.

The source notes that sampling can break deterministic loops and produce multiple plausible continuations. (Source: MASTER_Mathematical_Cheatsheet_All_Lecture_PDFs_v2)

2.5.18 Temperature Is Not Sampling

This distinction is critical.

Temperature:

\boxed{ z_i\rightarrow z_i/T }

changes the distribution.

Sampling:

\boxed{ y_t\sim P }

actually introduces randomness.

Therefore:

\boxed{ T=0 \rightarrow \text{greedy} }

in the implementations described by your sources, while:

T>0

normally means scale logits and then sample. (Source: Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch))

2.5.19 Top-k + Temperature

Now combine:

\boxed{ \text{Top-k}+\text{Temperature}+\text{Sampling}. }

This is the exact combination implemented in Raschka's generation function. (Source: Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch))

The source implementation effectively does:

model
 ↓
last-position logits
 ↓
top-k filter
 ↓
temperature scaling
 ↓
softmax
 ↓
multinomial sampling
 ↓
next token

Then append and repeat.

2.5.20 Let's Follow the Actual Code Logic

The source generation function does:

logits = model(idx_cond)
logits = logits[:, -1, :]

The second line means:

We only care about the model's distribution for the next token, which corresponds to the final sequence position.

Then:

torch.topk(...)

performs top-k filtering.

Then:

logits = logits / temperature

performs temperature scaling.

Then:

probs = torch.softmax(...)

converts the filtered logits into probabilities.

Then:

torch.multinomial(...)

samples the next token. (Source: Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch))

2.5.21 Complete Next-Token Mathematical Pipeline

We can write the source logic as:

z_t=f_\theta(y_{<t})

then top-k masking:

\tilde z_{t,i} = \begin{cases} z_{t,i},&i\in S_k\\ -\infty,&i\notin S_k \end{cases}

then temperature:

\hat z_{t,i} = \frac{\tilde z_{t,i}}{T}

then:

P(y_t=i) = \frac{e^{\hat z_{t,i}}} {\sum_j e^{\hat z_{t,j}}}

then:

\boxed{ y_t\sim P. }

Then:

y_{<t+1} = (y_{<t},y_t).

And repeat.

2.5.22 Where Does Top-p Fit?

Top-p performs a different type of filtering.

Instead of:

|S|=k,

it constructs:

S_p

such that:

\boxed{ \sum_{i\in S_p}P_i\ge p }

with S_p minimal.

So a combined stochastic decoder can use:

\boxed{ \text{temperature + top-k} }

or:

\boxed{ \text{temperature + top-p} }

or, depending on the system:

\boxed{ \text{top-k + top-p + temperature}. }

Your decoding slides recommend temperature plus top-k and/or top-p for tasks such as chat, stories, and brainstorming. (Source: decoding-sampling-slides)

2.5.23 Repetition Penalty

There is another control in your master cheatsheet:

\boxed{\text{Repetition penalty}}

The purpose is different from top-k/top-p.

Top-k/top-p says:

Remove low-ranked candidates.

Repetition penalty says:

Make already-generated tokens less attractive.

Your master cheatsheet summarizes the slide formulation as:

\boxed{ z_i\leftarrow\frac{z_i}{\theta} \quad\text{for previously seen tokens, with }\theta>1 }

before softmax. (Source: MASTER_Mathematical_Cheatsheet_All_Lecture_PDFs_v2)

Correction: Dividing a negative repeated logit by theta>1 increases it. The supplied rule suppresses positive logits; sign-aware implementations multiply negative logits instead.

Complete grounded review

2.5.24 Why Does This Reduce Repetition?

Suppose two candidate tokens have:

z_A=4

and:

z_B=2.

Suppose A already appeared.

Take:

\theta=2.

Then:

z_A'=\frac42=2.

while:

z_B'=2.

Now they tie.

Before penalty:

4>2.

After penalty:

2=2.

So A's advantage has been reduced.

Thus:

\boxed{ \theta>1 \Rightarrow \text{previously seen tokens receive reduced logits under this slide formulation}. }

2.5.25 Repetition Penalty vs Top-k

These solve different problems.

Repetition penalty

Changes the score of certain tokens based on history.

Top-k

Changes which tokens remain eligible based on current rank.

Top-p

Changes which tokens remain eligible based on current probability mass.

Therefore:

\boxed{ \text{History-based control} \neq \text{distribution truncation}. }

2.5.26 A Full Combined Numerical

Let's create an exam-style question.

Suppose the current logits are:

Token 1 has already appeared.

Use:

T=2,

We will follow the conceptual order:

\text{repetition penalty} \rightarrow \text{top-k} \rightarrow \text{temperature} \rightarrow \text{softmax} \rightarrow \text{sample}.
Step 1 — Repetition penalty

Token 1:

Other logits unchanged:

Step 2 — Top-3

The top three are:

[1.25,2,1,-\infty].

[-0.75,0,-1].

2.5.27 What If We Had No Repetition Penalty?

Original:

z=[5,4,2,0].

Top 3:

[5,4,2,-\infty].

Temperature T=2:

[2.5,2,1,-\infty].

Softmax:

\approx[0.5465,0.3315,0.1220,0].

With repetition penalty:

[0.2567,0.5434,0.1999,0].

The previously seen first token loses its dominance.

That is exactly what the penalty is intended to do.

2.5.28 Important Caveat About Repetition Penalty

The exact repetition-penalty implementation can vary across libraries and systems.

For this course, use the formulation given in your supplied slides/cheatsheet:

\boxed{ z_i/\theta,\qquad \theta>1 }

for previously seen tokens.

Do not silently substitute another library's exact variant into a source-specific numerical question.

2.5.41 Another Mixed Exam Question

Why could top-p retain 3 tokens in one context and 30 in another, even with the same p=0.9?

Because:

\boxed{ p }

controls cumulative probability mass rather than candidate count.

A sharply concentrated distribution reaches 0.9 quickly.

A flat distribution requires many tokens to reach 0.9.

2.5.42 Another Mixed Exam Question

Which strategy is better when exact reproducibility is required?

From your slides:

\boxed{\text{Greedy decoding}}

because it is deterministic. (Source: decoding-sampling-slides)

2.5.43 Another Mixed Exam Question

Which strategy is more appropriate when many different outputs can be valid?

The source guidance is:

\boxed{ \text{sampling with temperature + top-k/top-p} }

for tasks such as chat, storytelling, and brainstorming. (Source: decoding-sampling-slides)

2.5.44 Another Mixed Exam Question

Why is beam search often paired with length normalization?

Because:

\boxed{ \text{raw sequence probability decreases as more token probabilities are multiplied}. }

Without normalization, longer candidates can be unfairly penalized merely because they contain more factors below 1.

The source explicitly motivates the length penalty this way. (Source: decoding-sampling-slides)

2.5.45 One Subtle but Very Important Distinction

You should now distinguish:

Model quality

The model produces:

P(y_t|y_{<t},x).
Decoder policy

The decoder decides:

\boxed{\text{what to do with that distribution}.}

Therefore:

\boxed{ \text{Decoder }\neq\text{ model}. }

The same model can generate very different outputs depending on:

T,\;k,\;p,\;B,

and other decoding controls.

Greedy
\boxed{ z \rightarrow \arg\max \rightarrow y_t }

No stochastic sampling.

\boxed{ z \rightarrow Temperature \rightarrow Softmax \rightarrow Sort \rightarrow Cumulative\ Sum \rightarrow TopP \rightarrow Renormalize \rightarrow Sample }

2.5.49 Complete Pipeline for Beam Search

\boxed{ z_1 \rightarrow \text{candidate sequences} \rightarrow \text{score} \rightarrow \text{prune} \rightarrow z_2 \rightarrow \text{expand again} \rightarrow\cdots }

2.5.51 The Five Numerical Patterns You Must Master

At this point, your exam preparation should automatically recognize these five patterns.

Pattern 1 — Temperature
z\rightarrow z/T\rightarrow softmax.
Pattern 2 — Top-k
\text{rank}\rightarrow\text{keep }k\rightarrow-\infty\rightarrow softmax.
Pattern 3 — Top-p
\text{sort probabilities}\rightarrow \text{cumulative}\rightarrow \ge p\rightarrow \text{renormalize}.
Pattern 4 — Beam
\text{expand}\rightarrow \text{multiply/log-sum}\rightarrow \text{keep top }B.
Pattern 5 — Length normalization
\boxed{ s(Y)= \frac{\log P(Y)} {(5+|Y|)^\alpha/6^\alpha} }

2.5.52 Exam Trap Summary

Trap 1

Top-k is not:

\text{top }k\%\text{ vocabulary}.

It is:

\boxed{k\text{ tokens}}.
Trap 2

Top-p is not:

p\%\text{ vocabulary}.

It is:

\boxed{\text{probability mass }p}.
Trap 3

Top-p cumulative sum does not have to equal exactly p.

It must first become:

\boxed{\ge p}.
Trap 4

Temperature does not remove tokens.

\boxed{ T=\text{distribution reshape} }
Trap 5

Top-k does not choose the final token.

It restricts the sampling set.

Trap 6

Beam width B is not top-k k.

\boxed{ B=\text{number of sequences} }
\boxed{ k=\text{number of next-token candidates} }
Trap 7

Raw sequence probability and length-normalized score are not the same.

2.5.53 Final Mental Model

Think of the model as saying:

"Here are the possibilities."

Then the decoder asks:

Greedy

Which one is best right now?

Sampling

Let's draw one according to the probabilities.

Temperature

How sharply should I favor the likely choices?

Top-k

Only let the best k choices participate.

Top-p

Let enough high-probability choices participate to cover probability mass p.

Beam

Keep several possible futures alive and compare their sequence scores.

Length normalization

Don't let sequence length unfairly dominate beam scoring.

Repetition penalty

Reduce the attractiveness of tokens that have already appeared.

That is the complete conceptual map.
