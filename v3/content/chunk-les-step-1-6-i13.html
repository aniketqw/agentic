Motivation

Control dimension-driven score variance before normalization, making score comparisons less extreme under a clear statistical model.

Prerequisites

  • Dot products
  • Variance of independent sums
  • Softmax derivative

Notation and units

  • S=QK^T/sqrt(d_k)
  • A_ij row coefficient
  • d_k is Q/K head width

Assumptions

  • Components zero-mean unit-variance and independent across c; Q and K components independent for variance derivation
  • Scaling is an architecture choice, not a guarantee for every learned distribution

Step-by-step derivation

  1. Let s=sum_c q_c k_c. Under the stated independence, E[q_c k_c]=0 and Var(q_c k_c)=E[q_c²]E[k_c²]=1.
  2. Cross-coordinate covariance vanishes under independence, so Var(s)=sum 1=d_k and SD=sqrt(d_k).
  3. Dividing by sqrt(d_k) gives unit variance; dividing by d_k gives variance 1/d_k, which shrinks with width.
  4. The row-softmax Jacobian is diag(a)-aa^T. Near one-hot distributions, many of its entries approach zero; the effect on the full loss gradient depends on the downstream objective.
  5. For raw [2,3], weights [.2689414214,.7310585786]; [20,30] gives [.0000453979,.9999546021]. Their ordering matches but concentration differs.
  6. Scaled book scores [1.2705,1.8524,1.8111,1.0795,.5577,1.5440]/sqrt(2) normalize across six keys before weighted V aggregation; source values are rounded, not regenerated weights.

Why this matters

Large raw dot products can produce sharply saturated softmax weights. Scaling controls their characteristic size under a useful initialization model.

Prerequisites: Expectation and population variance, Three projections: Q, K, and V

Symbols, shapes and assumptions

For this variance derivation assume independent coordinate products qrkr, with q and k independent, each mean zero and variance one. Learned activations need not satisfy these assumptions exactly.

Derivation or mechanism

  1. Each qrkr has mean zero and second moment one.
  2. Independent coordinate products make Var(Σqrkr)=dk.
  3. Dividing the dot product by √dk divides its variance by dk, giving one under the stated assumptions.
\mathrm{Var}(q^\top k)=d_k,\qquad\mathrm{Var}(q^\top k/\sqrt{d_k})=1

Worked numerical

For dk=4, the raw score variance is 4 in this model. Dividing the score by 2 changes its variance to 4/2²=1. The statement is about variance across random coordinates, not a guarantee that every numerical score equals one.

Edge cases and limits

  • Correlation, nonzero means, or different variances change the derivation.
  • Scaling does not guarantee unsaturated softmax for every trained input.

Step 1.6 — Attention Scores → Why Divide by \sqrt{d_k}?

Plan node: I1 · chat order #13

We now have the core self-attention objects:

Q=XW_Q,\qquad K=XW_K,\qquad V=XW_V

and we know what they mean:

Q=\text{query representations}
K=\text{key representations}
V=\text{value representations}

The next operation is:

\boxed{S=QK^T}

These are the raw attention scores.

The next question is one of the most important mathematical questions in Transformers:

Why don't we directly apply softmax to QK^T? Why do we divide by \sqrt{d_k}?

Your Transformer slides explicitly state that the scaling keeps scores in a healthy range so softmax does not saturate.

Raschka gives the same rationale: as the key/query dimension becomes large, dot products can become large; softmax then behaves more like a step function, producing very small gradients.

We will now derive why.

1.6.1 Start With the Raw Score

For query q_i and key k_j:

\boxed{ s_{ij}=q_i^Tk_j }

If:

q_i,k_j\in\mathbb R^{d_k}

then:

q_i^Tk_j = \sum_{r=1}^{d_k}q_{ir}k_{jr}

So a dot product is actually a sum of d_k individual products.

For example, if d_k=4:

q^Tk = q_1k_1+q_2k_2+q_3k_3+q_4k_4

1.6.2 The Hidden Problem: The Dimension Controls the Size

Suppose the components of q and k are roughly:

\mathbb E[q_r]=0
\mathbb E[k_r]=0

and:

\operatorname{Var}(q_r)\approx1
\operatorname{Var}(k_r)\approx1

with the usual simplifying independence assumption.

Then:

q_rk_r

has approximately:

\operatorname{Var}(q_rk_r)=1.

Since:

q^Tk=\sum_{r=1}^{d_k}q_rk_r

the variance of the sum is approximately:

\operatorname{Var}(q^Tk) \approx d_k.

Therefore:

\boxed{ \operatorname{Std}(q^Tk)\approx\sqrt{d_k} }

This is the key mathematical observation.

Important source distinction

The uploaded Transformer slides/book explicitly give the scaling and its purpose, but the variance derivation above is a supplementary mathematical derivation explaining the reason behind that choice. The source itself says scaling prevents softmax saturation and small gradients.

1.6.3 Let's See What Happens as d_k Gets Larger

Imagine:

d_k=4

Then the standard deviation of the raw dot product is roughly:

\sqrt{4}=2

Now:

d_k=64

gives:

\sqrt{64}=8

And:

d_k=1024

gives:

\sqrt{1024}=32

So larger query/key dimensions naturally produce a wider range of dot-product scores.

That creates trouble for softmax.

1.6.4 Why Is a Large Score Range Bad for Softmax?

Recall:

\operatorname{softmax}(s_i) = \frac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}}

Suppose:

s=[1,2,3]

Softmax gives a reasonably distributed result.

But consider:

s=[1,20,40]

Then:

e^{40}

is enormously larger than:

e^{20}

which is enormously larger than:

e^1.

So softmax becomes approximately:

[0,0,1].

The distribution becomes extremely sharp.

This is called softmax saturation.

Correction: A saturated softmax has small Jacobian entries; this does not by itself prove softmax-plus-cross-entropy gradients vanish, since they simplify to p-y.

Complete grounded review

1.6.5 Why Is Saturation Bad?

The model needs gradients to learn.

For softmax:

p_i=\frac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}}

the derivative is:

\boxed{ \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial s_j} = p_i(\delta_{ij}-p_j) }

where:

\delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1&i=j\\ 0&i\neq j \end{cases}

Now suppose:

p=[0.00001,0.00001,0.99998]

For the winning class:

p_3\approx1

so:

p_3(1-p_3)\approx0.

For the losing classes:

p_i\approx0.

Therefore their gradients are also tiny.

So:

\boxed{ \text{very large score differences} \rightarrow \text{softmax saturation} \rightarrow \text{tiny gradients} }

This is exactly the training problem highlighted in Raschka's explanation.

1.6.6 The Solution

If the raw dot product has standard deviation approximately:

\sqrt{d_k},

we can normalize it by dividing by:

\sqrt{d_k}.

Thus:

\boxed{ s_{ij} = \frac{q_i^Tk_j}{\sqrt{d_k}} }

and in matrix form:

\boxed{ S= \frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} }

This keeps the score variance approximately around a constant scale under the simplified assumptions.

That prevents the score magnitudes from growing simply because the head dimension became larger.

1.6.7 Why Specifically \sqrt{d_k}, Not d_k?

This is a common interview question.

We established:

\operatorname{Var}(q^Tk)\approx d_k.

To normalize a random variable's scale, divide by its standard deviation:

\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(q^Tk)} \approx\sqrt{d_k}.

So:

\boxed{ \text{variance}\sim d_k \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{standard deviation}\sim\sqrt{d_k} }

and therefore:

\boxed{ \frac{q^Tk}{\sqrt{d_k}} }

is the natural scaling.

This is the deeper mathematical reason behind the familiar formula.

1.6.8 Compare d_k=4 vs d_k=64

Suppose the raw scores are roughly:

d_k=4

Typical magnitude:

\sim2
d_k=64

Typical magnitude:

\sim8

Without scaling, softmax sees much larger numbers for d_k=64.

With scaling:

\frac{8}{\sqrt{64}} = \frac8{8} = 1

So we bring the scale back to approximately the same range.

Thus:

\boxed{ \text{scaling removes the artificial dependence on head dimension} }

1.6.9 Now Use the Exact Example From the Book

Recall from Step 1.5:

q_2^T K = [1.2705,\;1.8524,\;1.8111,\;1.0795,\;0.5577,\;1.5440]

The example uses:

d_k=2.

Therefore:

\sqrt{d_k} = \sqrt2 \approx1.4142.

We divide every score by this value.

\frac{1.2705}{\sqrt2} \approx0.8984
Score 2

\boxed{ S_{\text{scaled}} \approx [0.8984,\;1.3098,\;1.2806,\;0.7633,\;0.3944,\;1.0918] }

Raschka uses precisely this scaling step before applying softmax.

1.6.11 Now Softmax

Next:

A=\operatorname{softmax}(S_{\text{scaled}})

giving approximately:

\boxed{ A= [0.1500,\;0.2264,\;0.2199,\;0.1311,\;0.0906,\;0.1820] }

The book reports these attention weights for the worked example.

Check:

0.1500+0.2264+0.2199+0.1311+0.0906+0.1820 \approx1.

So now we have a proper normalized weighting distribution.

1.6.12 What Changed Conceptually?

Before scaling:

q_i^Tk_j

means:

raw compatibility between query i and key j.

After scaling:

\frac{q_i^Tk_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}

we have the same compatibility idea but on a numerically controlled scale.

After softmax:

a_{ij} = \operatorname{softmax} \left( \frac{q_i^Tk_j}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right)

we have normalized attention weights.

So the progression is:

\boxed{ \text{raw compatibility} \rightarrow \text{scaled compatibility} \rightarrow \text{normalized weight} }

1.6.13 Why Do the Weights Sum to 1?

Softmax guarantees:

a_{ij}\geq0

and:

\sum_j a_{ij}=1.

Therefore each row of:

A

is a distribution over the keys available to that query.

Your Transformer slides explicitly describe this: every row is turned into a probability-like normalized distribution whose entries sum to 1.

A useful terminology distinction:

These are attention weights. They behave mathematically like a distribution, but they are not necessarily calibrated probabilities of a real-world event.

1.6.14 Why Row-Wise?

Take:

S\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}.

Rows correspond to queries.

Columns correspond to keys.

So:

S_{ij}

means:

compatibility of query position i with key position j.

We therefore normalize within each row:

A_{i,:} = \operatorname{softmax}(S_{i,:}).

That gives each query its own attention budget:

\sum_jA_{ij}=1.

1.6.15 Think of Each Row as a Budget

Suppose:

A_{2,:} = [0.1,0.6,0.2,0.1].

The current query says:

10% → token 1
60% → token 2
20% → token 3
10% → token 4

So the query allocates its entire attention budget:

10+60+20+10=100\%.

Then these weights are used to mix the corresponding values.

1.6.16 What Comes After Softmax?

Now we finally use V.

The output for query i is:

\boxed{ o_i=\sum_j a_{ij}v_j }

In matrix form:

\boxed{ O=AV }

So the full chain becomes:

\boxed{ Q,K,V \rightarrow QK^T \rightarrow \frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \rightarrow \operatorname{softmax} \rightarrow AV }

Your Transformer slides give exactly:

S=\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}, \qquad A=\operatorname{softmax}(S), \qquad O=AV.

1.6.17 Why Does AV Work?

Suppose:

A_i=[0.2,0.5,0.3]

and:

v_1=[1,0]
v_2=[0,2]
v_3=[3,1].

Then:

o_i = 0.2v_1+0.5v_2+0.3v_3

Compute each component:

First:

0.2(1)+0.5(0)+0.3(3) = 1.1

Second:

0.2(0)+0.5(2)+0.3(1) = 1.3

Therefore:

\boxed{ o_i=[1.1,1.3] }

So attention literally creates a weighted combination of values.

Raschka describes the resulting context vector as a weighted sum of the value vectors.

              divide by √d_k
                    ↓
                  softmax
                    ↓
        [a_i1, a_i2, ..., a_in]
                    ↓
        weighted sum of values
                    ↓
                   o_i

That is self-attention.

1.6.19 Why the Name "Scaled Dot-Product Attention"?

Now the name makes complete sense.

Dot-product

Because:

q_i^Tk_j

is used to calculate compatibility.

Scaled

Because:

\frac{q_i^Tk_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}

is used instead of the raw dot product.

Attention

Because the resulting normalized weights determine how much information each token receives from the others.

Thus:

\boxed{ \text{Scaled Dot-Product Attention} }

1.6.20 One Formula Contains Everything

The complete single-head attention equation is:

\boxed{ \operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax} \left( \frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right)V }

This is not a formula you should memorize blindly.

Now you can reconstruct it:

1. Compare
QK^T
2. Control scale
\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}
3. Turn scores into normalized weights
\operatorname{softmax}(\cdot)
4. Use those weights to mix information
(\cdot)V

Therefore:

\boxed{ \text{match} \rightarrow \text{scale} \rightarrow \text{normalize} \rightarrow \text{retrieve/mix} }

The attention handbook gives the same decomposition and emphasizes that keys determine the weights while values provide the content being mixed. (Source: Understanding_Attention_Handbook_01)

1.6.21 A Very Important Numerical Insight

Suppose two scores are:

s_1=2,\qquad s_2=3.

Their difference is:

1.

Softmax is sensitive to this difference.

But suppose the scores become:

s_1=20,\qquad s_2=30.

The difference is now:

10.

Then softmax overwhelmingly favors the second.

Scaling reduces the magnitude of the scores so that these comparisons don't become unnecessarily extreme as d_k grows.

So the scaling is not changing which score is larger in a basic positive-scalar sense.

It is controlling how sharply softmax responds to those differences.

1.6.22 An Important Property

Because:

\sqrt{d_k}>0,

dividing by it does not change the ordering:

q_i^Tk_1>q_i^Tk_2

still implies:

\frac{q_i^Tk_1}{\sqrt{d_k}} > \frac{q_i^Tk_2}{\sqrt{d_k}}.

So scaling does not change the ranking of matches.

It changes the temperature-like sharpness of the softmax response.

This is why we can say:

the scale stabilizes the distribution without fundamentally changing the relative ordering of the raw compatibility scores.

1.6.23 Connection to Our Earlier Temperature Concept

Earlier we learned:

\operatorname{softmax}(z/T)

where:

  • smaller T → sharper distribution,
  • larger T → flatter distribution.

Now:

\operatorname{softmax} \left( \frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right)

can mathematically be viewed as applying a fixed scaling to the score vector.

So increasing d_k without compensation would make scores larger in typical magnitude and therefore make softmax sharper.

Dividing by \sqrt{d_k} offsets that effect.

This is a useful connection between the probability foundation and attention.

1.6.24 Tensor Shapes — Complete This Step

Given:

X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}
W_Q,W_K\in\mathbb R^{d\times d_k}
W_V\in\mathbb R^{d\times d_v}

we have:

Q,K\in\mathbb R^{n\times d_k}
V\in\mathbb R^{n\times d_v}

Then:

QK^T : (n\times d_k)(d_k\times n) \rightarrow n\times n

Then:

\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \in\mathbb R^{n\times n}

Then:

A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}

Finally:

AV : (n\times n)(n\times d_v) \rightarrow n\times d_v

Therefore:

\boxed{ O\in\mathbb R^{n\times d_v} }

The same shape progression is given in your Transformer mathematical cheat sheet and Transformer slides. (Source: Step1_Transformers_Mathematical_Cheatsheet)

1.6.25 Batch Version

With batch size B:

X\in\mathbb R^{B\times n\times d}.

Then usually:

Q,K\in\mathbb R^{B\times n\times d_k}
V\in\mathbb R^{B\times n\times d_v}

and:

QK^T

becomes a batched matrix multiplication producing:

\boxed{ B\times n\times n }

followed by:

B\times n\times n \rightarrow B\times n\times d_v.

Later, when we introduce multiple heads, this becomes something like:

\boxed{ B\times h\times n\times d_k }

which is why tensor-shape understanding is so important.
