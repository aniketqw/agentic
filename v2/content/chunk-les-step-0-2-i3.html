Why this matters

Logits need to become a normalized distribution without overflowing when all scores are large.

Prerequisites: Matrix multiplication and shape contracts

Symbols, shapes and assumptions

Normalize over the vocabulary for token prediction, or over allowed key positions for attention. Temperature τ must be positive. At least one entry must be finite.

Derivation or mechanism

  1. Choose m=max(z).
  2. Subtract m from every logit before dividing by τ. The common exponential factor cancels.
  3. Compute log Z from these shifted exponentials. Subtract log Z to obtain log-probabilities.
p_i=\frac{\exp((z_i-m)/\tau)}{\sum_j\exp((z_j-m)/\tau)}
\log p_i=(z_i-m)/\tau-\log\sum_j\exp((z_j-m)/\tau)

Worked numerical

For logits [1000,1001,1002] and τ=1, subtract 1002 to obtain [-2,-1,0]. The probabilities are approximately [0.090031,0.244728,0.665241]. Adding or subtracting 1000 leaves them unchanged. For [0,-1000], log-softmax retains [0,-1000] even if exp(-1000) underflows to zero.

Edge cases and limits

  • τ=0 is not the softmax formula; a greedy limit is a separate procedure.
  • All -∞ means no allowed outcome, so the companion rejects the row.
  • The numerical implementation is stable for finite, representable input differences; it is not arbitrary-precision arithmetic.

Connections

  • F10 derives the softmax/CE gradient.
  • T04 applies row softmax to scaled attention scores.
  • Later decoding cards compare truncation rules.

New teaching capsule and stated examples only; inherited prose/formulas/code remain pending

Why this matters

A language model scores a sequence through conditional next-token predictions; this explains the training target and the sequence objective.

Prerequisites: Stable softmax and log-softmax

Symbols, shapes and assumptions

p(x₁,…,xT) factors into conditional probabilities. The first factor needs an initial context/BOS convention. This factorization does not assume independent tokens.

Derivation or mechanism

  1. Apply the conditional probability chain rule.
  2. Take a logarithm to convert the product into a sum.
  3. Negate that sum for negative log likelihood; averaging introduces an explicit denominator.
p(x_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(x_t\mid x_{<t})
-\log p(x_{1:T})=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p(x_t\mid x_{<t})

Worked numerical

If the two conditional probabilities are 0.4 and 0.25, the joint probability is 0.1. Its negative natural log is 2.302585093. The two losses are 0.916290732 and 1.386294361; their sum agrees. Their mean is a different quantity, 1.151292546.

Edge cases and limits

  • Zero assigned probability for an observed token gives infinite negative log likelihood.
  • Do not compare a sequence-sum loss with a token-mean loss without accounting for length.

Connections

  • T01 implements shifted teacher-forced targets.
  • KD and RL later change which distribution supplies the sampled sequence.

STEP 0.2 — PROBABILITY → LOG-PROBABILITY → SOFTMAX

Plan node: I0 · chat order #3

Now we move from the linear-algebra primitives we just learned into the probability machinery that turns Transformer outputs into meaningful distributions.

This step is extremely important because the same mathematical ideas will later reappear in:

attention → language-model training → decoding → distillation → KL divergence → RL/PPO/GRPO.

The master cheatsheet explicitly places these formulas at the mathematical foundation of the Transformer and language-model objective. (Source: Step1_Transformers_Mathematical_Cheatsheet)

0.2.1 First: What problem are we trying to solve?

Suppose the Transformer has processed:

The cat is

and now must predict the next token.

The model does not directly output:

"sleeping"

Instead, the final layer produces a vector of arbitrary real numbers called logits:

z = \begin{bmatrix} 2.1\\ 0.3\\ 4.2\\ 1.0\\ ... \end{bmatrix}

There is one logit for every vocabulary token.

For example:

Token Logit
the 2.1
dog 0.3
sleeping 4.2
ran 1.0
... ...

But these numbers are not probabilities.

They can be:

  • negative
  • positive
  • larger than 1
  • smaller than 0

So we need a transformation:

\boxed{\text{logits} \rightarrow \text{probabilities}}

That transformation is softmax.

0.2.2 What is a probability distribution?

Suppose we have five possible next tokens.

A probability distribution could be:

p=[0.05,0.10,0.60,0.05,0.20]

A valid discrete probability distribution must satisfy two conditions:

p_i\ge 0

and

\sum_i p_i=1

So:

0.05+0.10+0.60+0.05+0.20=1

This means:

All possible outcomes share 100% of the probability mass.

This is exactly the kind of distribution a language model needs when choosing its next token.

The supplied MiniLLM material gives a concrete example where the next-token distribution is:

[0.05,0.10,0.60,0.05,0.20]

for {the, cat, sat, dog, ran}. (Source: minillm-slides)

0.2.3 Why does a language model need probabilities?

Because language generation is inherently uncertain.

Take:

The sky is

Possible continuations might be:

P(\text{blue})=0.60
P(\text{cloudy})=0.15
P(\text{clear})=0.10
P(\text{dark})=0.05

etc.

The model is effectively saying:

Given the context, how likely is each possible next token?

Mathematically:

\boxed{P(x_t\mid x_{<t})}

where:

  • x_t = current/next token
  • x_{<t} = all previous tokens

This conditional probability is the heart of autoregressive language modeling.

The NLP with Transformers book describes GPT-style language modeling exactly this way: the model estimates the probability of a token sequence through conditional next-token probabilities. (Source: Natural language processing with Transformers _ building)

0.2.4 Autoregressive probability

Suppose the sentence is:

I love cats

Let the tokens be:

x_1=I,\quad x_2=love,\quad x_3=cats

We want the probability of the whole sequence:

P(I,love,cats)

Using the chain rule of probability:

\boxed{ P(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n) = \prod_{t=1}^{n} P(x_t\mid x_{<t}) }

So:

P(I,love,cats) = P(I)\, P(love\mid I)\, P(cats\mid I,love)

This is called autoregressive factorization.

It is in your cheatsheet as the language-model objective. (Source: Step1_Transformers_Mathematical_Cheatsheet)

0.2.5 Why factorization matters

Calculating the probability of every possible full sentence directly would be impossible.

Instead, the model breaks the problem into manageable pieces:

\text{whole sequence}

becomes

\text{next-token prediction at position 1}
\text{next-token prediction at position 2}
\text{next-token prediction at position 3}

etc.

So a Transformer doesn't need to directly model:

P(\text{"The cat sat on the mat"})

as one giant indivisible object.

It models:

P(\text{The})

then

P(\text{cat}\mid\text{The})

then

P(\text{sat}\mid\text{The cat})

and so on.

This is the foundation of both training and generation.

0.2.6 The problem with multiplying probabilities

Consider:

0.8\times0.7\times0.9\times0.6\times0.5
=0.1512

Now imagine a sequence of 1,000 tokens.

You might multiply hundreds or thousands of numbers like:

0.001\times0.02\times0.4\times0.003\times\cdots

The result becomes extremely tiny.

This creates numerical problems.

So we use logarithms.

0.2.7 Log-probability

Take:

P(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \prod_t P(x_t\mid x_{<t})

Apply \log:

\log P(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \log \left( \prod_t P(x_t\mid x_{<t}) \right)

Using:

\log(ab)=\log a+\log b

we obtain:

\boxed{ \log P(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \sum_t \log P(x_t\mid x_{<t}) }

So multiplication becomes addition.

That is enormously more convenient computationally.

Example

Suppose:

P_1=0.8
P_2=0.7
P_3=0.9

Then:

P_{\text{sequence}} = 0.8(0.7)(0.9) = 0.504

Log form:

\log(0.504)

which is equivalent to:

\log(0.8)+\log(0.7)+\log(0.9)

So instead of multiplying many tiny values, we add log-probabilities.

0.2.8 Why are log-probabilities negative?

Because for probabilities:

0<p\le1

and therefore:

\log p\le0

For example:

\log(1)=0
\log(0.5)\approx-0.693
\log(0.1)\approx-2.303
\log(0.001)\approx-6.908

So:

Higher probability → log probability closer to 0.

Lower probability → more negative log probability.

This gives us a very useful way to measure how well the model predicted the correct token.

0.2.9 Negative log-probability

Because log probabilities are negative, training usually minimizes:

\boxed{ -\log P(x_t\mid x_{<t}) }

This is the negative log-likelihood loss for one token.

Look at what happens:

Model predicts correct token with probability 0.9
-\log(0.9)\approx0.105

Very small loss.

Model predicts it with probability 0.5
-\log(0.5)\approx0.693

Larger loss.

Model predicts it with probability 0.01
-\log(0.01)\approx4.605

Huge loss.

Therefore:

\boxed{\text{better probability for the correct token} \Rightarrow \text{smaller loss}}

The cheatsheet expresses the per-position loss as:

L_t=-\log P_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})

and sequence-average loss as the mean over positions. (Source: Step1_Transformers_Mathematical_Cheatsheet)

0.2.10 Now we reach SOFTMAX

We have logits:

z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_V

where V is vocabulary size.

We need to convert them into:

p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_V

such that:

p_i\ge0

and:

\sum_i p_i=1

Softmax does exactly this.

\boxed{ \operatorname{softmax}(z_i) = \frac{e^{z_i}} {\sum_j e^{z_j}} }

This formula appears directly in the mathematical cheatsheet. (Source: Step1_Transformers_Mathematical_Cheatsheet)

0.2.11 Understand softmax from the inside out

Suppose:

z=[1,2,3]
Step 1: exponentiate
e^1\approx2.718
e^2\approx7.389
e^3\approx20.086

So:

[e^1,e^2,e^3] \approx [2.718,7.389,20.086]
Step 2: calculate the sum
2.718+7.389+20.086 = 30.193
Step 3: divide each by the total
p_1=\frac{2.718}{30.193}\approx0.090
p_2=\frac{7.389}{30.193}\approx0.245
p_3=\frac{20.086}{30.193}\approx0.665

Therefore:

\boxed{ \operatorname{softmax}([1,2,3]) \approx [0.090,0.245,0.665] }

Check:

0.090+0.245+0.665\approx1

0.2.12 Why the exponential?

This is an important WHY, not a memorization detail.

Softmax uses:

e^{z_i}

for several useful reasons.

1. It guarantees positivity

Exponentials are always positive:

e^z>0

So the resulting probabilities cannot be negative.

2. It preserves ordering

If:

z_3>z_2

then:

e^{z_3}>e^{z_2}

So larger logits still produce larger probabilities.

3. It amplifies differences

Compare:

z=[1,2]

Difference = 1.

Exponentials:

[e^1,e^2]=[2.718,7.389]

Now the larger value dominates much more strongly.

This gives softmax a useful competitive behavior.

4. It normalizes

Dividing by:

\sum_j e^{z_j}

forces the results to sum to 1.

So softmax is essentially:

\boxed{ \text{positive transformation + normalization} }

0.2.13 Logits are NOT probabilities

This distinction is essential.

Suppose:

z=[2,1,-1]

These are logits.

You cannot say:

"Token 1 has probability 2."

That is impossible.

After softmax:

\operatorname{softmax}(z)

produces valid probabilities.

So:

\boxed{ \text{Logit} \neq \text{Probability} }

Pipeline:

\boxed{ \text{Transformer hidden state} \rightarrow \text{linear projection} \rightarrow \text{logits} \rightarrow \text{softmax} \rightarrow \text{probabilities} }

0.2.14 A very important Transformer connection

Remember our earlier attention equation:

S=QK^T

These are also just scores.

They are not probabilities.

Then:

A=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}+M\right)

produces attention weights.

The attention handbook describes the process as:

project → compare → scale → constrain → normalize → aggregate.

In particular, row-wise softmax turns the compatibility scores into nonnegative weights that sum to one across allowed keys. (Source: Understanding_Attention_Handbook_01)

So softmax appears in two extremely important places:

Attention
\boxed{\text{scores}\rightarrow\text{attention weights}}
Output layer
\boxed{\text{logits}\rightarrow\text{token probabilities}}

Same mathematical operation.

Different semantic purpose.

0.2.15 Attention softmax vs language-model softmax

This distinction is worth memorizing.

Suppose a sequence has n=4 tokens.

Attention scores for token 3 might be:

[1.2,\;2.0,\;0.4,\;-\infty]

After causal masking and softmax:

[0.31,\;0.69,\;0.00,\;0.00]

These tell us how the query distributes its attention over allowable positions.

But at the output layer we might have:

z=[2.1,0.7,4.3,-1.2,\ldots]

Softmax turns these into probabilities over the vocabulary.

Thus:

Softmax location Distribution is over
Attention positions/tokens being attended to
Output layer vocabulary tokens

This distinction will become very important later when we study decoding.

0.2.16 Causal masking + softmax

Suppose we have:

S= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 5\\ 3 & 2 & 4\\ 1 & 4 & 2 \end{bmatrix}

For a causal language model:

Token 1 cannot look at future tokens 2 and 3.

Token 2 cannot look at future token 3.

So we use a mask:

M= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\infty & -\infty\\ 0 & 0 & -\infty\\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}

Therefore:

S+M = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -\infty & -\infty\\ 3 & 2 & -\infty\\ 1 & 4 & 2 \end{bmatrix}

Now apply softmax row-by-row.

For row 1:

\operatorname{softmax}[2,-\infty,-\infty] = [1,0,0]

For row 2:

\operatorname{softmax}[3,2,-\infty]

gives approximately:

[0.731,0.269,0]

The -\infty effectively produces zero probability.

So the causal mask works because:

e^{-\infty}\rightarrow0

This is the mathematical mechanism behind preventing future-token information leakage.

0.2.17 Softmax has a beautiful property: shift invariance

Suppose:

z=[1,2,3]

Now add 10 to every number:

z'=[11,12,13]

Softmax gives exactly the same result:

\boxed{ \operatorname{softmax}(z+c) = \operatorname{softmax}(z) }

Why?

Because:

\frac{e^{z_i+c}} {\sum_j e^{z_j+c}}

can be rewritten as:

\frac{e^c e^{z_i}} {e^c\sum_j e^{z_j}}

and e^c cancels.

This gives us a very useful computational trick.

0.2.18 Numerically stable softmax

Instead of calculating:

\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}

we usually compute:

\boxed{ \operatorname{softmax}(z) = \operatorname{softmax}(z-\max(z)) }

Example:

z=[1000,1001,1002]

Directly computing e^{1000} can overflow numerically.

Instead subtract 1002:

[-2,-1,0]

Then:

\operatorname{softmax}([1000,1001,1002]) = \operatorname{softmax}([-2,-1,0])

Same probabilities, much safer numerically.

0.2.19 Temperature

Now introduce another concept that will become central in decoding.

Temperature modifies softmax:

\boxed{ p_i= \frac{e^{z_i/T}} {\sum_j e^{z_j/T}} }

where:

T>0

Case 1: T=1

Normal softmax.

Case 2: T<1

The distribution becomes sharper.

Example:

T=0.5

Logits become effectively:

[2z_1,2z_2,\ldots]

Differences get amplified.

The highest-probability token tends to dominate.

Case 3: T>1

The distribution becomes flatter.

Differences between logits matter less.

More probability spreads to lower-ranked alternatives.

Intuition

Think of temperature as a control over certainty:

\boxed{ T\downarrow \Rightarrow \text{sharper} }
\boxed{ T\uparrow \Rightarrow \text{flatter} }

This will later become a major text-decoding mechanism.

0.2.20 One-hot targets

Now connect probability to training.

Suppose vocabulary:

[\text{the},\text{cat},\text{sat},\text{dog},\text{ran}]

The correct next token is:

sat

Represent the target as a one-hot vector:

y=[0,0,1,0,0]

Suppose the model predicts:

q=[0.05,0.10,0.60,0.05,0.20]

So:

q_{\text{sat}}=0.60

The loss is:

\boxed{ L=-\log(0.60) }
L\approx0.5108

This exact worked example appears in the supplied MiniLLM material. (Source: minillm-slides)

Notice something interesting:

The model predicted probabilities for all five tokens, but for a one-hot target the loss ultimately depends on the probability assigned to the correct token.

0.2.21 Why negative log probability is such a good loss

Suppose the correct answer is sat.

Prediction A
P(\text{sat})=0.9

Loss:

-\log(0.9)=0.105
Prediction B
P(\text{sat})=0.5

Loss:

-\log(0.5)=0.693
Prediction C
P(\text{sat})=0.01

Loss:

-\log(0.01)=4.605

This means the loss heavily punishes confidently wrong predictions.

That's exactly what we want.

A model saying:

"I am 99% sure the answer is wrong"

should be punished much more strongly than a model saying:

"I am only somewhat uncertain."

0.2.22 From one token to the entire sentence

For n tokens:

L_t=-\log P_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})

Average:

\boxed{ L = -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^{n} \log P_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t}) }

This is the standard next-token training objective shown in your cheatsheet. (Source: Step1_Transformers_Mathematical_Cheatsheet)

Therefore the complete training chain is:

\boxed{ \text{tokens} \rightarrow \text{Transformer} \rightarrow \text{logits} \rightarrow \text{softmax} \rightarrow \text{probabilities} \rightarrow -\log P(\text{correct token}) \rightarrow \text{loss} }

That chain is one of the most important chains in the entire course.

0.2.23 Connection to attention

Now connect today's lesson to yesterday's linear algebra.

Yesterday:

Q=XW_Q
K=XW_K
V=XW_V

then:

S=QK^T

Today we add:

A=\operatorname{softmax}(S/\sqrt{d_k}+M)

and:

O=AV

So we have:

\boxed{ X \rightarrow Q,K,V \rightarrow QK^T \rightarrow \text{scaled scores} \rightarrow \text{softmax} \rightarrow A \rightarrow AV }

The attention handbook explicitly emphasizes that the attention weights are intermediate coefficients and that the actual output is AV. (Source: Understanding_Attention_Handbook_01)

This is why yesterday's dot product was not the end of the story.

0.2.24 Extremely important distinction

There are now three different kinds of numbers you must not mix up:

1. Learned parameters

For example:

W_Q

These are model parameters learned during training.

2. Scores / logits

Examples:

QK^T

or final vocabulary logits:

z

These are raw compatibility/evidence scores.

3. Normalized weights/probabilities

Examples:

A=\operatorname{softmax}(QK^T)

or:

p=\operatorname{softmax}(z)

These are normalized values.

Raschka explicitly distinguishes learned weight parameters from dynamic attention weights. (Source: Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch))

0.2.26 Tensor-shape grounding

Let's make this precise.

Suppose:

X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}

Then:

Q=XW_Q

with:

W_Q\in\mathbb R^{d\times d_k}

so:

Q\in\mathbb R^{n\times d_k}

Likewise:

K\in\mathbb R^{n\times d_k}

Therefore:

QK^T

has shape:

(n\times d_k)(d_k\times n)

giving:

\boxed{n\times n}

Then:

A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}

and if:

V\in\mathbb R^{n\times d_v}

then:

AV = (n\times n)(n\times d_v)

giving:

\boxed{n\times d_v}

These exact shape relationships are summarized in your Transformer cheatsheet. (Source: Step1_Transformers_Mathematical_Cheatsheet)

0.2.27 A tiny complete numerical example

Let's make the entire softmax calculation concrete.

Suppose the model produces three logits:

z=[2,1,0]

Exponentials:

[e^2,e^1,e^0]
\approx[7.389,2.718,1]

Sum:

7.389+2.718+1=11.107

Therefore:

p_1=\frac{7.389}{11.107}\approx0.665
p_2=\frac{2.718}{11.107}\approx0.245
p_3=\frac{1}{11.107}\approx0.090

So:

\boxed{ p\approx[0.665,0.245,0.090] }

Suppose the correct token is token 2.

Then:

L=-\log(0.245)
\boxed{L\approx1.407}

So the model receives a substantially larger penalty than it would if the correct token had probability 0.9.

0.2.28 PyTorch implementation

This is the computation you should be able to recognize immediately in code.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

# Raw logits from a model
logits = torch.tensor([2.0, 1.0, 0.0])

# Convert logits to probabilities
probs = F.softmax(logits, dim=-1)

print("Logits:", logits)
print("Probabilities:", probs)
print("Sum:", probs.sum())

# Suppose token 2 is the correct answer
target = torch.tensor(1)

# Negative log probability
loss = -torch.log(probs[target])

print("Probability of correct token:", probs[target])
print("Loss:", loss)

Conceptually:

logits
   ↓
softmax
   ↓
probabilities
   ↓
select correct-token probability
   ↓
-log(...)
   ↓
loss

0.2.29 A safer practical version

In real PyTorch training, you usually don't manually do:

softmax()
-log()

for the language-model loss.

Instead, functions such as cross-entropy loss are designed to operate directly on logits in a numerically stable way.

That is one reason the next lesson—entropy, cross-entropy, and KL divergence—is so important.

0.2.30 What you should understand, not memorize

At this point you should be able to explain:

Why probability?

Because the model needs to represent uncertainty over possible next tokens.

Why logits?

Because the neural network naturally produces unconstrained real-valued scores.

Why softmax?

Because we need to transform those scores into nonnegative normalized values.

Why log?

Because sequence probabilities are products, and logarithms convert products into sums while improving numerical behavior.

Why negative log probability?

Because we want high probability on the correct answer to correspond to low loss.

Why does softmax appear in attention?

Because raw compatibility scores need to become normalized coefficients before we aggregate value vectors.

0.2.31 One subtle point: attention weights are not automatically "probabilities" in the ordinary semantic sense

Mathematically, a row of attention weights has the properties:

a_i\ge0

and

\sum_i a_i=1

so it behaves like a distribution over positions.

But we should be careful with interpretation.

It does not automatically mean:

"The model believes there is a 70% probability that this token is the reason for the prediction."

The attention handbook specifically warns against automatically treating attention weights as faithful explanations of model decisions. (Source: Understanding_Attention_Handbook_01)

This distinction will matter later when you study Transformer interpretability.

You can now see why our first two mathematical lessons were ordered this way.

We started:

because embeddings, queries, keys, and values are vectors.

Then:

because projections and batched transformations are matrix operations.

Then:

\boxed{\text{dot product}}

because it creates compatibility scores.

Now:

\boxed{\text{probability}}

because scores must often become normalized distributions.

Then:

\boxed{\text{log probability}}

because language-model sequence probabilities are products.

Then:

because it converts raw scores into normalized weights/probabilities.

Next we need to understand:

\boxed{ \text{Entropy} \rightarrow \text{Cross-Entropy} \rightarrow \text{KL Divergence} }

because those concepts are the mathematical bridge from ordinary language-model training to:

knowledge distillation → RLHF → RLVR → PPO → GRPO → DeepSeek-R1.
