Step 5.26 — Indic Language Benchmarks: Why IndicXTREME Exists

Plan node: I10 · chat order #67

We now transition from tool-using reasoning models to the Indic-language part of your material.

The first thing to understand is not the list of tasks.

It is the underlying problem:

\boxed{ \text{Why isn't a generic multilingual benchmark enough for Indian languages?} }

The supplied IndicXTREME deck begins with exactly this question. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

5.26.1 The assumption that sounds reasonable

Suppose we have a multilingual model such as XLM-R.

A natural assumption is:

\boxed{ \text{strong English model} + \text{multilingual pretraining} \Rightarrow \text{good Indian-language performance} }

This is the idea of zero-shot transfer.

The model learns representations from high-resource languages and is expected to transfer those capabilities to low-resource languages.

Conceptually:

\text{English training} \rightarrow \text{shared multilingual representation} \rightarrow \text{Hindi/Bengali/Tamil/etc.}

But the IndicXTREME material says that this breaks down for many of India's languages. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

5.26.2 Why does zero-shot transfer break down?

The deck identifies three separate problems.

We should understand them individually.

Problem 1 — Poor model coverage

The first problem is:

\boxed{ \text{the model may not adequately support the language at all} }

The deck notes that among the 22 scheduled Indian languages, only 15 are supported by XLM-R.

It gives examples such as:

\boxed{\text{Bodo, Dogri, Kashmiri}}

being absent from resources such as CC-100 and mC4 used in multilingual pretraining. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

This is a fundamental issue.

You cannot transfer useful representations for a language that the model barely encountered or did not represent in its pretraining resources.

5.26.3 Think of model capacity as a budget

Suppose a model has a finite representational capacity:

C.

It must distribute that capacity across languages:

C=C_1+C_2+\cdots+C_L.

For a dominant language such as English:

C_{\text{English}}

can be very large.

For a very low-resource language:

C_{\text{low-resource}}

may be tiny.

Therefore:

\boxed{ \text{multilingual} \neq \text{equally represented} }

This is an important conceptual point from the deck's “starved languages” discussion. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

The equation above is a conceptual model, not a formula presented by the source.

5.26.4 Problem 2 — Even supported languages are starved

Suppose a language technically exists in the pretraining corpus.

That does not mean it receives enough data.

The second problem is:

\boxed{ \text{language exists} \neq \text{language has sufficient representation} }

The deck says that even when low-resource languages are present, their pretraining data can be dramatically smaller than English.

That affects both:

\boxed{\text{model capacity}}

and:

\boxed{\text{vocabulary representation}}.

(Source: indicxtreme-slides)

5.26.5 Why vocabulary matters

Consider a language that is poorly represented in the tokenizer.

A word may be split into many subword pieces:

w \rightarrow t_1,t_2,t_3,t_4,t_5.

Another language might represent common words much more efficiently:

w' \rightarrow t_1,t_2.

More fragmented tokenization can make sequences longer and potentially make modeling less efficient.

The IndicXTREME deck specifically mentions poor allocation of vocabulary alongside model capacity for low-resource languages. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

The precise downstream effect of tokenization efficiency is general ML reasoning; the slide itself does not quantify that effect.

5.26.6 Problem 3 — The benchmark itself doesn't cover the languages

This is perhaps the biggest reason a dedicated benchmark is necessary.

Suppose a model claims:

“I support 22 Indian languages.”

But the benchmark evaluates only a few of them.

Then:

\boxed{ \text{claim of multilingual capability} }

is not adequately tested.

The deck notes that in XTREME-R:

  • only 3 of 10 tasks cover more than two Indic languages;
  • no task covers more than seven;
  • 15 of the 22 constitutional languages appear in no task at all. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

So there is a measurement problem:

\boxed{ \text{language capability} \neq \text{language capability that has actually been benchmarked} }

5.26.7 The three-problem model

This gives us the foundation of IndicXTREME:

\boxed{ \text{Poor model coverage} }
+
\boxed{ \text{Insufficient data for supported languages} }
+
\boxed{ \text{Poor benchmark coverage} }
\Downarrow
\boxed{ \text{Need a dedicated Indic benchmark} }

This is the conceptual starting point of the deck. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

5.26.8 What exactly is IndicXTREME trying to cover?

The deck describes three related contributions from the original work:

\boxed{\text{IndicCorp v2}}
\boxed{\text{IndicXTREME}}
\boxed{\text{IndicBERT v2}}

(Source: indicxtreme-slides)

Let's understand why these three belong together.

IndicCorp v2

A monolingual Indic pretraining corpus:

\boxed{20.9\text{ billion tokens}}

across:

\boxed{24\text{ languages}}

and:

\boxed{4\text{ language families}}.

Its purpose is to provide better Indic-language pretraining resources. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

IndicXTREME

The benchmark contains:

\boxed{9\text{ tasks}}

across:

\boxed{20\text{ languages}}

with human supervision.

The deck says there are:

\boxed{105\text{ evaluation sets}}

in total, of which:

\boxed{52}

are brand-new contributions. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

IndicBERT v2

A:

\boxed{278\text{M-parameter}}

BERT model pretrained on IndicCorp v2 and evaluated using IndicXTREME.

So the logical chain is:

\boxed{ \text{better Indic data} \rightarrow \text{Indic-specialized model} \rightarrow \text{Indic-specific evaluation} }

(Source: indicxtreme-slides)

5.26.9 Why are there 24 languages in IndicCorp but 22 scheduled languages?

This is a useful place to be careful with source terminology.

The deck says the paper targets the:

\boxed{22\text{ languages of the 8th Schedule}}

but IndicCorp v2 itself contains:

\boxed{24\text{ languages}}.

Later, the deck notes that IndicXTREME can evaluate only 19 of IndicCorp's 24 languages, leaving five without an evaluation set. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

So do not collapse all the numbers into “24 Indian languages” or “22 benchmark languages.”

They describe different scopes:

\boxed{ 22 = \text{constitutional target} }
\boxed{ 24 = \text{IndicCorp v2 corpus scope} }
\boxed{ 20 = \text{benchmark scope stated on the contribution slide} }

and later:

\boxed{ 19 = \text{languages actually evaluable according to the deck's limitation slide} }

That distinction is source-important.

5.26.10 Another critical concept: language-resource imbalance

The deck uses the taxonomy from Joshi et al. to classify languages according to resource availability.

At the extremes:

\boxed{5=\text{resource-rich “Winner”}}

and:

\boxed{1=\text{almost entirely unresourced “Left-Behind”}}.

Among languages newly added to IndicXTREME, the deck says:

\boxed{9}

are class-1 Left-Behinds, while only:

\boxed{3}

are class-5 Winners. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

5.26.11 Why is this classification useful?

Because “22 Indian languages” sounds homogeneous.

They are not.

The data available for:

\text{Hindi}

can be radically different from:

\text{Santali}

or:

\text{Bodo}.

Therefore the benchmark should not only ask:

\boxed{ \text{How good is the model overall?} }

It should also ask:

\boxed{ \text{How does performance change as language resources decrease?} }

That becomes important later when the deck compares Winners vs Left-Behinds. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

5.26.12 Numerical understanding

Suppose, purely as an illustrative abstraction, that a multilingual model has an effective language-resource budget of:

1000

units.

Suppose English consumes:

500

units, while five low-resource languages collectively receive:

50.

Then average allocation to those five languages is:

\frac{50}{5} = \boxed{10}

units each.

This does not represent the actual model's resource allocation—the slide provides no such numeric budget. It is a conceptual numerical illustration of the imbalance problem.

5.26.13 Exam numerical: benchmark coverage

The deck states:

22

constitutional languages, but:

15

appear in no XTREME-R task.

Then benchmark-covered languages under that statement:

22-15 = \boxed{7}.

This matches the slide's statement that no XTREME-R task covers more than seven Indic languages. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

The important lesson is not merely the number 7.

It is:

\boxed{ \text{a benchmark can systematically hide failures by omitting languages} }

5.26.14 Exam numerical: corpus growth

IndicCorp v1:

8.79\text{B tokens}.

IndicCorp v2:

20.9\text{B tokens}.

The ratio is:

\frac{20.9}{8.79} \approx \boxed{2.38\times}.

The deck reports exactly this value and notes that the paper rounds it to about 2.3×. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

This is an excellent exam-style calculation.

5.26.15 Exam numerical: post-filter reduction

Before offensive-content filtering:

23.1\text{B tokens}.

After filtering:

20.9\text{B tokens}.

Reduction:

23.1-20.9 = \boxed{2.2\text{B tokens}}.

Percentage:

\frac{2.2}{23.1}\times100 \approx \boxed{9.52\%}.

The deck reports this as approximately:

\boxed{9.5\%}.

(Source: indicxtreme-slides)

5.26.16 The key benchmarking philosophy

Now we can formulate the problem properly.

A multilingual benchmark should not merely ask:

\text{“Can the model do NLP?”}

It should ask:

\boxed{ \text{“Can the model do NLP across languages with drastically different resource levels?”} }

And for Indic languages, the benchmark needs:

\boxed{ \text{broad language coverage} + \text{human-supervised data} + \text{multiple task types} }

The deck's next section therefore moves into the 9-task IndicXTREME suite. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

5.26.17 One subtle distinction to remember

There are really three different problems:

\boxed{\text{Data problem}}

Does the model have enough training text?

\boxed{\text{Representation problem}}

Does the model/tokenizer adequately represent the language?

\boxed{\text{Evaluation problem}}

Do we actually have a benchmark that measures the capability?

IndicXTREME primarily attacks the third problem, while the overall paper also contributes IndicCorp v2 to address the first and IndicBERT v2 to demonstrate the value of Indic-focused pretraining. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)

Step 5.26 checkpoint

The entire motivation can now be compressed to:

\boxed{ \text{High-resource multilingual pretraining} \not\Rightarrow \text{equal Indic-language capability} }

because:

\boxed{ \text{some languages are missing} }
\boxed{ \text{some have very little data} }
\boxed{ \text{some are barely benchmarked} }

Therefore:

\boxed{ \text{Indic-specific data + Indic-specific benchmark} }

is necessary to expose the real performance gaps. (Source: indicxtreme-slides)
