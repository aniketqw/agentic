Step 5.35 — IndicGenBench: Qualitative Failure Analysis

Plan node: I11 · chat order #76

We now move from:

\boxed{\text{“How badly does the model perform?”}}

to the more important diagnostic question:

\boxed{\text{“What exactly goes wrong?”}}

The IndicGenBench authors manually inspected model outputs to understand the failure modes behind the quantitative gaps.

The supplied deck focuses on three major failure patterns:

\boxed{\text{Hallucination}}
\boxed{\text{Language bleeding}}
\boxed{\text{Polysemy / meaning-selection errors + missing information}}

(Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.1 Why qualitative analysis is necessary

Suppose a model gets:

\text{chrF}=40.

That tells us the output is imperfect.

But it does not tell us why.

The model could be failing because of:

\text{wrong facts}

or:

\text{wrong language}

or:

\text{wrong word meaning}

or:

\text{missing information}.

So:

\boxed{ \text{quantitative score} + \text{qualitative inspection} }

gives a more useful diagnosis.

5.35.2 How did the authors perform the qualitative analysis?

The deck says the authors manually reviewed:

\boxed{20}

predictions from:

\boxed{\text{PaLM-2-L}}

for each of:

\boxed{\text{CrossSum-IN}}

and:

\boxed{\text{FLORES-IN}}

across:

\boxed{5\text{ languages}}.

(Source: indicgenbench-slides)

So the analysis examines actual outputs rather than only aggregate benchmark scores.

5.35.3 Numerical interpretation of the sample size

For one task:

20\text{ predictions/language} \times 5\text{ languages} = \boxed{100}

manually reviewed predictions.

For both tasks:

100\times2 = \boxed{200}.

So the qualitative inspection covers:

\boxed{200\text{ predictions}}

under this reading of the deck's sampling description.

This is a derived calculation; the slide states the per-task/per-language counts rather than explicitly writing “200.”

5.35.4 Failure Mode 1 — Hallucination

The first major failure is:

\boxed{\text{hallucination}}

The deck says that in cross-lingual summarization, the model regularly invents details that are absent from the source article. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

This is particularly serious for summarization.

5.35.5 What should a summary do?

Suppose source document D contains facts:

F(D)=\{f_1,f_2,\ldots,f_n\}.

A faithful summary should primarily compress information already supported by D:

\boxed{ S\approx \text{compressed representation of }D }

It should not introduce an unsupported:

f_{n+1}\notin D.

The source doesn't provide this mathematical formulation; it is a conceptual way to describe the failure.

5.35.6 Example of hallucination

Imagine the source article says:

“The spacecraft was scheduled to launch next month.”

A bad summary might say:

“The spacecraft successfully launched last week.”

The second statement introduces at least two unsupported changes:

\text{scheduled} \rightarrow \text{successful}

and:

\text{next month} \rightarrow \text{last week}.

The model has generated a plausible sentence that is not faithful to the source.

That is exactly the kind of issue the qualitative analysis is intended to uncover. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.7 Why is hallucination especially problematic in low-resource generation?

The benchmark already showed:

\boxed{ \text{Indic generation} < \text{English generation} }

and:

\boxed{ \text{Low-resource} < \text{Medium} < \text{High} }.

Hallucination gives us a concrete mechanism behind part of that poor generation quality.

A score decrease is now connected to:

\boxed{ \text{failure to preserve source-grounded facts} }.

5.35.8 Hallucination vs ordinary wording difference

Do not confuse:

\boxed{\text{paraphrasing}}

with:

\boxed{\text{hallucination}}.

Suppose source:

“The mission was delayed twice.”

Valid paraphrase:

“The launch was postponed on two occasions.”

Same factual content.

But:

“The mission was delayed five times.”

changes the factual content.

So:

\boxed{ \text{surface change} \neq \text{factual error} }

and qualitative analysis is needed to distinguish them.

5.35.9 Exam question

Question:

Why can a generation metric alone fail to fully diagnose hallucination?

Because a numerical similarity score tells us how similar the generated output is to the reference under that metric, but qualitative inspection is needed to determine whether the model introduced unsupported facts that were not in the source. The IndicGenBench authors explicitly use manual analysis to expose such errors. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.10 Failure Mode 2 — Language Bleeding

Now comes a very interesting multilingual failure:

\boxed{\text{language bleeding}}

The deck focuses on:

\boxed{\text{Awadhi}}
\boxed{\text{Haryanvi}}
\boxed{\text{Chhattisgarhi}}

which are related to Hindi and share its:

\boxed{\text{Devanagari script}}.

(Source: indicgenbench-slides)

When translating into these languages, the model frequently:

\boxed{\text{mixes in Hindi words}}

and produces:

\boxed{\text{incorrectly inflected verb forms}}.

(Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.11 What does language bleeding mean?

Suppose the requested output language is:

L_T=\text{Awadhi}.

But the model generates:

\text{Awadhi} + \text{Hindi}

instead of pure Awadhi.

So:

\boxed{ P(\text{target-language token}) }

is being contaminated by a closely related language's patterns.

This is a conceptual interpretation of the observed behavior.

5.35.12 Why are related languages especially vulnerable?

Because they may share:

\boxed{\text{script}}

and:

\boxed{\text{linguistic structure}}

but still have different:

\boxed{\text{vocabulary}}

and:

\boxed{\text{morphology}}

and:

\boxed{\text{inflection}}

rules.

So the model may learn:

“This input belongs to a Hindi-like region of representation space.”

but fail to maintain the precise target-language boundary.

The source explicitly identifies the shared-Devanagari/related-language setting and the resulting Hindi mixing. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.13 Important distinction: related language ≠ same language

Consider:

\boxed{ \text{Hindi} \neq \text{Awadhi} }

even if:

\text{related}

and:

\text{same script}.

Thus:

\boxed{ \text{shared script can help transfer} }

but can also create:

\boxed{ \text{cross-language contamination} }.

This is a very useful multilingual-model insight.

5.35.14 Language bleeding and tokenizer effects are different problems

Do not mix these up.

Tokenizer problem
\text{language} \rightarrow \text{too many subword tokens}

This affects:

\boxed{\text{context efficiency}}.
Language bleeding
\text{target language} \rightarrow \text{wrong related-language words/forms}

This affects:

\boxed{\text{generation correctness}}.

They can coexist, but they are different failure mechanisms.

5.35.15 Example of incorrect morphology

Suppose the target language requires:

\text{verb form }V_T.

The model instead generates:

V_H

following Hindi morphology.

Then even if:

\text{semantic meaning}

is roughly correct, the generated language is still wrong.

So:

\boxed{ \text{semantic adequacy} \neq \text{language correctness} }.

This is particularly important when evaluating generation in closely related languages.

5.35.16 Failure Mode 3 — Polysemy

The third failure is:

\boxed{\text{polysemy / word-sense selection}}

A single source word can have multiple meanings depending on context.

The deck gives a concrete example involving:

\boxed{\text{“hearing”}}

which can refer to a:

\text{court hearing}

or:

\text{listening}.

(Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.17 What went wrong?

In the Konkani example, the same English word appears in two different senses.

The model chooses the:

\boxed{\text{“listening”}}

sense both times.

But the first occurrence refers to:

\boxed{\text{a court hearing}}.

(Source: indicgenbench-slides)

So the model failed to use context to disambiguate the word.

5.35.18 Why is this important for machine translation?

Machine translation is not:

\boxed{ \text{word} \rightarrow \text{fixed dictionary translation} }

It should be:

\boxed{ \text{word + context} \rightarrow \text{appropriate target meaning} }.

This is the essence of polysemy.

The same source word:

w

can represent:

m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_k.

The model must estimate:

\boxed{ P(m_i\mid w,\text{context}) }

rather than always choosing the same m_i.

That equation is general NLP reasoning, not a source-provided formula.

5.35.19 Numerical polysemy example

Suppose a word has two possible senses:

P(m_1\mid context)=0.8
P(m_2\mid context)=0.2.

Correct interpretation:

\boxed{m_1}.

But if the model uses a context-insensitive prior:

P(m_1)=0.4,
P(m_2)=0.6,

it may choose:

\boxed{m_2}

incorrectly.

Again, this is an illustrative mathematical explanation, not a reported model probability.

5.35.20 Failure Mode 4 — Missing information

The same qualitative-analysis slide identifies another issue:

\boxed{ \text{information omission} }

The deck gives an Assamese example where:

\boxed{\text{“to shop”}}

is simply absent from the generated translation. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.21 What does omission mean mathematically?

Suppose source information contains:

F=\{f_1,f_2,f_3,f_4\}.

A complete translation should preserve all relevant information.

If the model produces:

\hat F=\{f_1,f_2,f_4\},

then:

\boxed{f_3}

has disappeared.

This is different from hallucination.

Hallucination
\boxed{ \hat F=F+\{f_{\text{false}}\} }
Omission
\boxed{ \hat F\subset F }

This distinction is extremely useful.

5.35.22 Four-way failure taxonomy

We can now organize the qualitative results:

Hallucination
\boxed{ \text{adds unsupported information} }
Language bleeding
\boxed{ \text{mixes target language with a related language} }
Polysemy error
\boxed{ \text{chooses the wrong sense of an ambiguous word} }
Omission
\boxed{ \text{drops information from the source} }

All four are explicitly represented in the final qualitative-analysis slides. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.23 Why these four failures are connected

Notice that these are not random errors.

They correspond to different requirements of generation:

\boxed{ \text{Faithfulness} }

requires avoiding hallucination.

\boxed{ \text{Target-language control} }

requires avoiding language bleeding.

\boxed{ \text{Contextual semantic understanding} }

requires correct polysemy resolution.

\boxed{ \text{Information preservation} }

requires avoiding omission.

So a good translation requires all four simultaneously.

5.35.24 Connect to the quantitative results

Earlier we observed:

\boxed{ \text{High resource} > \text{Medium} > \text{Low} }

and:

\boxed{ \text{English generation} > \text{Indic generation} }.

Qualitative analysis now tells us what some of those failures look like in practice:

\boxed{ \text{wrong facts} + \text{wrong language} + \text{wrong sense} + \text{missing content} }.

So:

\boxed{ \text{aggregate score} \rightarrow \text{diagnostic failure modes} }

is the important methodological connection.

5.35.25 Why related-language transfer is a double-edged sword

Earlier:

\boxed{ \text{Hindi exemplars} > \text{English exemplars} }

for many target languages. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

But now:

\boxed{ \text{Hindi} \rightarrow \text{Awadhi/Haryanvi/Chhattisgarhi} }

can cause Hindi to bleed into the target output. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

So linguistic similarity has two sides:

\boxed{ \text{similarity} \rightarrow \text{better transfer} }

but also:

\boxed{ \text{similarity} \rightarrow \text{greater risk of confusion} }.

That is a very important research insight.

5.35.26 Numerical conceptual model of this tradeoff

Suppose linguistic relatedness gives:

+10

points of transfer benefit.

But language confusion causes:

-4

points of bleeding-related degradation.

Then net:

10-4 = \boxed{+6}.

This is purely an illustrative decomposition.

The source does not provide separate numeric contributions for language bleeding.

The key idea is that relatedness can be both:

\boxed{\text{an advantage}}

and:

\boxed{\text{a source of interference}}.

5.35.27 Connect hallucination with summarization

CrossSum-IN is:

\boxed{ \text{long source} \rightarrow \text{short target output} }.

That compression creates a difficult requirement:

\boxed{ \text{remove redundancy without inventing facts} }.

The qualitative result shows that the model can fail specifically on the second requirement.

So summarization quality is not just:

\text{shorter text}.

It is:

\boxed{ \text{compression} + \text{faithfulness} }.

5.35.28 Connect polysemy to cross-lingual translation

In machine translation:

\text{source word} \rightarrow \text{target word}

is insufficient.

The actual process is closer to:

\boxed{ \text{source word} + \text{sentence context} + \text{discourse context} \rightarrow \text{target meaning} }.

The Konkani “hearing” example demonstrates a failure when context isn't used correctly to choose between senses. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.29 Connect omission to information preservation

For translation, a good system should preserve:

\boxed{ \text{who} + \text{what} + \text{when} + \text{where} + \text{relevant action} }.

If an important action such as:

\boxed{\text{“to shop”}}

disappears, the output may sound fluent while still being incomplete.

That's an especially important lesson:

\boxed{ \text{fluency} \neq \text{faithfulness}. }

The Assamese example in the deck demonstrates exactly this point. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.30 The qualitative-analysis matrix

You can organize the failures like this:

Failure What model does What should happen
Hallucination Adds unsupported fact Preserve source facts
Language bleeding Uses Hindi in another target language Stay in requested language
Polysemy Picks wrong word sense Use contextual sense
Omission Drops source information Preserve relevant content

(Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.31 Exam-style scenario

Suppose the source says:

“The court scheduled a hearing for Monday.”

Model translation interprets “hearing” as “listening.”

Classification:

\boxed{\text{Polysemy error}}

not hallucination.

Suppose instead the model writes:

“The court scheduled a hearing for Monday and the judge ordered five arrests.”

when the source says nothing about arrests.

Classification:

\boxed{\text{Hallucination}}.

Suppose:

“The boy went to the shop and bought food.”

becomes:

“The boy went to the shop.”

Classification:

\boxed{\text{Omission}}.

Suppose the target is Awadhi but half the generated words are Hindi.

Classification:

\boxed{\text{Language bleeding}}.

These distinctions are directly grounded in the failure categories shown by the deck. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.32 Why this matters for model improvement

Different errors require different interventions.

Hallucination

Potential direction:

\boxed{ \text{better source grounding / factuality control} }
Language bleeding

Potential direction:

\boxed{ \text{better target-language conditioning / language-specific training} }
Polysemy

Potential direction:

\boxed{ \text{better contextual semantic modeling} }
Omission

Potential direction:

\boxed{ \text{better coverage/content preservation} }

These are general remediation ideas. The supplied deck identifies the failure modes but does not prescribe a complete solution for each, so these improvement directions should be treated as model-level inference rather than source claims.

5.35.33 Final section: licensing

The deck closes with a practical but important issue:

\boxed{ \text{dataset licensing} }.

Because IndicGenBench builds on existing datasets, it inherits their licensing constraints. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

The reported licenses are:

\boxed{ \text{XQuAD-IN, FLORES-IN} \rightarrow \text{CC BY-SA 4.0} }
\boxed{ \text{CrossSum-IN} \rightarrow \text{CC BY-NC-SA 4.0} }
\boxed{ \text{XorQA-IN} \rightarrow \text{MIT} }

(Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.34 Why does source lineage affect licensing?

Because:

\boxed{ \text{new benchmark} }

doesn't necessarily mean:

\boxed{ \text{new legal status}. }

If a benchmark extends:

\text{dataset A},

its derived data may inherit constraints from dataset A.

So:

\boxed{ \text{technical lineage} \rightarrow \text{licensing lineage} }.

The deck explicitly calls this another consequence of the translation-based approach. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.35 Why should engineers care?

Suppose you build:

\text{new multilingual dataset}

from:

\text{existing benchmark}.

Even if:

\text{your annotations are new},

you still need to check:

\boxed{ \text{license of original data} }.

A dataset being publicly available does not automatically mean unrestricted commercial or derivative use.

The exact legal obligations depend on the license.

The deck's point is specifically that benchmark lineage determines these constraints. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)

5.35.37 The deepest takeaway

The most important lesson from the qualitative analysis is:

\boxed{ \text{A multilingual model can produce fluent-looking text and still fail in several fundamentally different ways.} }

It can:

\boxed{ \text{invent facts} }

while sounding fluent.

It can:

\boxed{ \text{mix languages} }

while remaining understandable.

It can:

\boxed{ \text{choose the wrong meaning} }

while producing grammatical text.

And it can:

\boxed{ \text{omit important source information} }

while generating a concise output.

Therefore:

\boxed{ \text{good generation} \neq \text{fluency alone} }

A robust multilingual generator needs:

\boxed{ \text{faithfulness} + \text{language fidelity} + \text{semantic correctness} + \text{information preservation} }.

That is the most important conclusion from the final IndicGenBench slides. (Source: indicgenbench-slides)
